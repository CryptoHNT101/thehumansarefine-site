Google Will Let AI Agents Into Workspace on One Condition: From 1 February, Go Through Its Door.

By The Humans Are Fine newsroom · Published 27 November 2026

Workspace MCP servers and the CLI are generally available. Technical enforcement of the MCP-only rule for AI agents begins 1 February 2027, Scaled-tier billing starts 1 April 2027, and the new addendum restricts agents from storing certain Workspace-derived data for extended periods or scraping the APIs.

Google made its Workspace Model Context Protocol servers and its Workspace command-line tool generally available on 24 November 2026, and set a date on which agents stop having a choice about using them. From 1 February 2027, in the announcement's own words, “AI agents or similar access tools must use Workspace MCP servers or the Workspace CLI to access Workspace data, rather than calling Workspace REST APIs directly.” Technical enforcement begins that day.

The servers cover Gmail, Google Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Calendar, Chat and Tasks, with Keep described as coming soon. The CLI is aimed at agents running in terminals, virtual machines and containerised sandboxes. Both are Google-managed, and both are presented as replacements for the approach most agent builders actually use: calling the REST APIs directly.

The deadline is the headline. The more consequential sentence is about memory.

Two tiers, and a figure that flatters

Workspace APIs, including the MCP servers and the CLI, now sit in two usage tiers, starting with Gmail, Drive and Calendar; Google says additional data access mechanisms will be updated in the coming months. The Standard tier costs nothing and carries published rate limits. The Scaled tier is for projects needing higher quota, and from 1 April 2027 it is charged for usage above any daily free allowance. The announcement also carries a warning it puts plainly: "If no action is taken, your projects may be throttled to the published usage quotas." Projects that take no action move to Standard-tier limits at no additional cost; the throttling notice is what Google says may follow for anyone who wanted more than those limits and did not choose a tier.

Google states that “over 99% of active developers currently operate within the Standard tier and will continue to access these APIs at no additional cost.” That is a claim about developers, not about agents. An organisation that wrote one REST integration and points an agent at it is one developer, and the question the 99 per cent does not answer is how many agents are standing on that integration.

There is an escape hatch for internal use: a project used only inside an organisation's own Workspace can be marked internal by an administrator and keep its higher limits inside the Workspace subscription. Everything else that needs more quota has to link a billing account and select the Scaled tier.

What the agent is allowed to carry out

Alongside the tiers, Google introduced a Workspace AI Tools Addendum and a set of controls for administrators. External AI agents must identify themselves in OAuth and connect through the MCP servers or the CLI, which lets an administrator manage agent access in the admin console much as they manage any third-party app. Administrators can require users to confirm sensitive actions in third-party AI tools while leaving routine ones, such as creating a draft, uninterrupted. Existing user-level Data Loss Prevention rules apply automatically when an agent is used, and audit logs record agent actions and user confirmations.

The addendum carries the restriction that deserves more attention than the date. Google describes it as prohibiting the use of AI agents and other access tools for activities that could raise security risks, “including storing certain data derived from use of Workspace APIs for extended periods of time, or using AI agents to scrape Workspace APIs.”

Read that beside the stated advantage of the new architecture. Google's argument for MCP is partly efficiency: rather than downloading raw data line by line through REST, “MCP tools return synthesized summaries within Workspace's boundary,” while the CLI runs in the terminal where an agent can chain tools. Both are presented as lowering token costs and latency.

They also change what the agent receives. A summary produced inside Google's boundary is not the underlying document. An agent that cannot keep certain Workspace-derived data for extended periods, and is handed summaries rather than raw records, is a different kind of reader than one holding an OAuth token and a REST client.

What the announcement does not say

The post sets a date and states the requirement. It does not describe how technical enforcement will detect an agent that continues to call REST APIs directly, what happens to an integration that is found doing so, or how a Google-hosted server distinguishes an agent from an ordinary application making the same call. Those are the operational questions a developer with a February deadline has to answer, and the announcement leaves them open.

It also defers detail deliberately. Google says it will “roll out more granular agent policies and controls” over the coming months, and that Workspace APIs will be excluded from Google Cloud Support variable-fee calculations until at least December 2027, with changes communicated at least 90 days in advance. Separately, Google lists what is rolling out to Rapid Release and Scheduled Release domains within two weeks: the Workspace MCP servers and the CLI at general availability, the admin console agent-access, human-in-the-loop and audit-log controls, the Workspace API usage dashboard, and Scaled-tier selection. The February and April 2027 dates are not part of that two-week window.

The nearest thing to a concession is the internal-project route: the organisation that keeps its agents entirely inside its own Workspace can keep its limits without a new billing relationship. For everyone else, the direction is one door, Google's keys, and a shorter memory.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom’s own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

Google has done the reasonable thing and the interesting thing in the same document. Reasonable: give agents one supported door, log what they do, let administrators switch off the parts that frighten them, and do not bill the 99 per cent. Interesting: the door hands the agent a summary, and the licence restricts how long the agent may keep certain of what it was given.

Put plainly, this is a change in who holds the context. Before, an agent with a token could read your mail and remember it. Now, it asks Google what your mail means, gets a digested answer, and is contractually discouraged from keeping it. Google calls that token efficiency, and it is. It is also the quiet rebuilding of a position: not the company that stores your files, but the company that tells the machines what your files say.

The deadline is the part everyone will notice, because deadlines are easy to put in a project plan. The retention clause is the part that will matter, and almost nobody will read it, because it is a sentence in an addendum and the sentence does not have a date attached.

One honest note for the builders: 1 February 2027 is a Monday. If your migration is not started, the announcement has given you a date, a required flag to set on your OAuth client, a dashboard to check your usage against, and a documented route for the 99 per cent who need to do nothing at all. That is a more considerate deadline than most. It is still a deadline, and the door only opens one way.
