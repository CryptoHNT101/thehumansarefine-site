The ECB Says Europe Is Financing the AI Boom, Not Using It

An ECB executive board member reports that AI has added about 10 per cent to euro-area firms’ capital expenditure plans this year, while only 7 per cent of surveyed firms describe their own AI use as “significant”. Both numbers come from the central bank’s own surveys of what firms say. Neither measures what AI has actually produced.

Philip R. Lane, a member of the European Central Bank’s Executive Board, used a keynote in San Francisco on 30 October to describe how AI is reshaping the euro area economy. The speech is titled “AI adoption in Europe” and was delivered at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco’s Economic Research Symposium on AI and the economy. It is published on the ECB’s own site.

Its most quotable finding is a gap. On the spending side, Lane reports that AI has added about 10 per cent to firms’ capital expenditure plans this year. On the use side, he reports that only a small minority of firms say they are using AI in a way they would call significant.

Both of those are survey results. That distinction governs everything below, so it is worth stating plainly before the numbers: the ECB is reporting what firms told its surveyors about their own plans and their own behaviour. It is not reporting measured output, measured productivity, or measured adoption from any independent source.

What the spending figure actually measures

The 10 per cent figure comes from the ECB’s Corporate Telephone Survey. Lane’s sentence is: “In the ECB Corporate Telephone Survey, firms report that AI has added about 10 per cent to their capital expenditure plans this year.”

Three qualifiers sit inside that one sentence and each narrows it. It is firms reporting. It is about their plans, not their spending to date. And it is “about” 10 per cent, a rounded figure presented without a published margin of error in the speech text.

The aggregate picture Lane paints around it is consistent with a capital-goods boom rather than a productivity story. Digital investment in the euro area “has expanded by 73 per cent in real terms since 2014, increasing from 2.3 per cent to 3.3 per cent of GDP”. AI, he says, “has been the main driver of global goods trade over the last year”, with AI-related exports and imports growing quickly, especially in core data centre goods — hardware for compute, storage and networking.

What the use figure actually measures

The 7 per cent figure comes from a different instrument: the ECB’s Survey on the Access to Finance of Enterprises, or SAFE, a large-scale survey covering both large firms and small and medium-sized enterprises. Lane reports the fourth-quarter 2025 round:

“In the fourth quarter of 2025 most firms participating in the SAFE reported that they were using AI: 64 per cent of firms said they were using it either experimentally or moderately, while only 7 per cent of firms reported that their use was ‘significant’ (Chart 13).”

Quotation note: the speech prints the word “significant” in double quotation marks. Where that word falls inside a longer quoted sentence, this article renders it in single quotation marks, which is British nesting convention. No other character of the quoted sentence is altered.

So the honest summary of the ECB’s own survey data is not that European firms are ignoring AI. It is that most of them are using it lightly, and that the word “significant” is doing a lot of work in the sentence. Sixty-four per cent report some use. Seven per cent report substantial use. The speech does not define the boundary between “moderately” and “significant” in its text; that definition lives in the survey instrument, which the speech does not reproduce.

On why firms adopt at all, the same round gives reasons: 62 per cent cited improving business processes, 23 per cent cited innovation, research and development or expanding their product range, and 15 per cent cited reducing labour costs. Cost-cutting is the least-cited motive of the three, which cuts against the simplest version of the “AI replaces workers” narrative.

The experiment inside the survey

The most interesting thing in the speech is not either headline number. It is that the fourth-quarter 2025 SAFE round ran a field experiment on whether firms know what their competitors are doing.

“Chart 14a shows that firms underestimate the rate of AI adoption by their peer firms, with most firms underestimating the share of competitors that have invested in AI, some by as much as 50 percentage points.”

Lane reports that giving firms information about their peers’ AI investment plans “makes firms change their beliefs”. He uses this to argue that domestic competitive pressure, rather than fear of foreign competition, is what moves AI investment.

This is a survey-based behavioural result, not an observation of investment behaviour following the intervention. It establishes that firms hold incorrect beliefs about their peers, and that those beliefs are responsive to information. It does not establish that corrected beliefs produce investment.

Where the money appears to be going

Lane’s account of credit is the part that most directly supports the headline. He notes that “lending to AI-related firms has contributed substantially to aggregate credit growth”, and that in the euro area digital investment “has primarily been in intangible assets, while investment in data centres has constituted a relatively small share”.

Read together with the capex figure, the picture is of euro-area money flowing into the buildout and into intangible assets, while the share of firms reporting heavy use of the technology remains in single digits. That is Lane’s argument. It is an argument built on the ECB’s own survey instruments, and the central bank is both the analyst and the data owner here.

What these numbers do not establish

  • That European firms are not adopting AI. Sixty-four per cent report some use. The finding is about intensity, not participation.
  • That AI is not raising European productivity. Nothing in the speech measures output per hour, firm-level productivity, or revenue effects. Lane is describing investment and reported use.
  • That the 10 per cent capex figure is economy-wide spending. It is what surveyed firms say AI added to their plans, not a national accounts measure of AI investment.
  • That the gap is a European failure. The speech offers no comparable US or Chinese survey of self-reported use intensity, so the comparison it invites is not made in the document.
  • That credit is not reaching adopters. Lane reports that AI-related lending contributed substantially to aggregate credit growth; the speech does not publish a split between infrastructure borrowers and adopting firms.

What we could not establish

  • The sample size and fieldwork dates for the SAFE round behind the 64 and 7 per cent figures. The speech reports the quarter and the survey name, not the sample. We did not retrieve the underlying SAFE publication and do not attribute one to it.
  • The definition of “significant” use. The figure is only as meaningful as that threshold, and the speech text does not state it.
  • Whether the 10 per cent capex figure has been published before. Secondary coverage of this speech exists, including at least one crypto-news aggregator, which we read but did not rely on; it attributes a credit-growth figure to the speech that the speech text does not contain, and we do not repeat it.
  • Whether any independent evaluation corroborates the ECB’s survey results. We found none cited in the speech.

Why it is worth reporting anyway

A central bank describing its own economy’s AI uptake in its own surveys is a primary record of how the institution sees the problem, and central banks are about to make credit and supervisory judgements on the basis of that view. The numbers are self-reported and the analysis is the data owner’s. Both of those are reasons to read the speech closely, not reasons to ignore it.

The one line that survives every caveat is the narrowest: an ECB board member told an audience of economists that AI is adding materially to European investment plans while a small minority of European firms say they use it seriously. Whether that gap closes, and how fast, is not in the document.

Read together, the two numbers say something narrower and more useful than either alone: Europe is not short of AI capital. It is short of AI use.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom’s own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

Europe is putting money into AI and not, so far, getting much use out of it. That is roughly what the European Central Bank said on Friday, using its own surveys, which is the part to keep in view.

The spending number is the eye-catching one: firms say AI added about a tenth to their investment plans this year. The use number is the deflating one: two thirds of firms say they use AI a bit, and seven in a hundred say they use it seriously. Everything in between is the story.

The detail worth carrying around is a different one. European firms badly misjudge their own competitors — some by 50 percentage points — and when you tell them what their peers are actually doing, they change their minds. That is a strange and specific finding. It suggests the barrier to adoption may be less about the technology than about everyone assuming nobody else is using it either.

What this is not is proof that AI is doing nothing for Europe. Nobody measured output. They asked firms what they were doing and multiplied the answers by a survey. A central bank describing its own economy with its own instruments deserves to be read as exactly that.
