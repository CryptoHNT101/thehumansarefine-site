The UK Is Putting a DeepMind-Built Planning Tool in 15 Councils. The 58% Is the Government’s Own Number.

PlanAssist expands from three pilot councils to 15 for twelve months, with the government intending to offer it to every council in England by the end of 2027. The claimed 58 per cent cut in time on planning tasks, and the halving of routine householder processing from eight weeks to four, are the department’s own assessment. No methodology, sample size or independent evaluation is published alongside them.

The Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government published a press release on 30 October announcing that PlanAssist, an AI planning tool, will move out of a three-council pilot and into fifteen councils for twelve months. The release is bylined to the department and to the Housing Secretary, Angela Rayner. It also announces a change to who decides planning applications, which takes effect the following day.

Both parts matter, and the second one has more immediate legal consequence for an applicant than the first.

What is being deployed, and to whom

PlanAssist is described in the release as having been “developed with Google DeepMind, Google Cloud, Faculty and local authorities”, and as bringing “applications, policies and site constraints into one place for trained officers to review”. It is a tool for officers, not a decision-maker.

The rollout, in the release’s own words: “Following a successful trial in Camden, Barnet and Dorset Councils, the tool will now expand to 15 councils for 12 months, with the government intending to make it available to every council in England by the end of 2027.”

Three things in that sentence are firm: the three named pilot councils, the number fifteen, and the twelve-month period. The national target is stated as an intention, by a department, with a deadline at the end of a calendar year. The release does not say which fifteen councils, what the expansion will cost, how it will be procured, or whether councils may decline.

The efficiency claim, and who is making it

The central number in the release is the department’s own:

“Early testing cut time spent on key planning tasks by 58% - helping us halve processing times for routine householder applications from eight weeks to four.”

This article reports that figure as the Ministry’s assessment, because that is what it is. The release does not publish the methodology behind the 58 per cent, the number of applications measured, the councils or officers involved in the measurement, the baseline period, or any independent evaluation. We checked the page for each of those and found none of them present.

That is not a claim that the number is wrong. It is a statement about what a reader can verify. A department announcing that its own tool cut task time by 58 per cent, with no published method, is making a claim on its own authority — the same structure as a vendor case study, with the difference that the department is also the client and the policy owner.

The eight-to-four-week figure carries the same status. It is the department’s account of “routine householder applications”, and it describes a halving that the release attributes to the combination of the tool and the wider reforms being announced, not to PlanAssist alone. The sentence joins the two with “helping us”, which is a statement of contribution rather than of isolated effect.

The change that takes effect on 31 October

Separately from the tool, the release announces a National Scheme of Delegation. It states: “From today (Saturday 31 October) straightforward applications will be decided by expert planning officers rather than waiting for a planning committee slot, helping families, shopkeepers and pub landlords get decisions sooner.”

The release is dated 30 October 2026. 31 October 2026 is a Saturday, so the weekday is correct and the word “today” is not — at publication the change was still a day away. This article records the effect as beginning on 31 October.

What the change does is move decision authority for “straightforward” applications from elected planning committees to employed officers. The release frames this as speed and certainty for applicants and as freeing councillors “to focus on the most significant developments”. It does not define “straightforward”, and it does not say how a resident who disagrees with an officer’s decision is differently placed than one who disagrees with a committee’s.

What the release says about staffing

The department pairs the tool with recruitment. The release reports “420 newly recruited planners through government-backed programmes”, which it describes as surpassing “the government’s 2024 manifesto commitment to recruit 300 additional planners more than two years before the end of this Parliament”. Rayner is quoted going further: “we’re going further with 1,400 new recruits across the planning system by the end of this Parliament”.

The 420 and the 1,400 are different measures — planners recruited now, against a planned total by the end of a Parliament — and this article does not add them together or treat one as evidence of the other.

What the ministers and the vendor say

Rayner, quoted in the release: “Families wanting more space and businesses looking to grow shouldn’t be left waiting unnecessarily for straightforward planning decisions.”

The AI Minister, Kanishka Narayan: “This is what putting AI to work for the public looks like in practice. By slashing the time spent on routine planning tasks by more than half, we’re helping families get quicker answers while freeing up skilled planners to focus on the major developments that will get Britain building.”

Owen Larter, Senior Director for Frontier AI Policy and Global Affairs at Google DeepMind, said PlanAssist “puts AI to work by freeing planning officers to spend more time deciding on applications and less time on administrative paperwork”.

Note the structure of these quotations: the minister repeats the 58 per cent claim in stronger language (“more than half”) without adding a method, and the vendor describes the benefit in terms of officer time rather than decision quality or speed of outcome. Nothing in the release offers a measured effect on the quality or consistency of decisions.

What this story does not establish

  • That PlanAssist caused a 58 per cent reduction. The figure is the department’s own account of “early testing”, published without method, sample or baseline.
  • That householder applications now take four weeks. The release states a halving attributed to the tool and the reforms together, not a measured national processing time.
  • That decision quality is unchanged or improved. No measure of decision quality, appeal or overturn rates is published in the release.
  • That all fifteen councils have agreed. The release announces an expansion; it publishes no list and no confirmations. We did not contact any council.
  • That PlanAssist is mandatory. The release describes a tool made available to councils; it does not describe a mandate.

What we could not establish

  • Any published evaluation of the Camden, Barnet and Dorset pilot. We searched the release and found no reference to a report, methodology, sample size or independent assessment. Absence from the release is not proof that no such document exists elsewhere, and we did not locate one.
  • The definition of “straightforward” applications under the new delegation scheme, and whether it is set nationally or by each council.
  • Whether the twelve-month expansion includes a published evaluation point at which results would be released.
  • Independent corroboration of any figure in the release. The only secondary coverage we saw was trade press repeating the department’s numbers, which adds no independent measurement and is not relied on here.

Why the story is the expansion, not the tool

PlanAssist was announced earlier in 2026. What is new on 30 October is scale, a national target and a same-week transfer of decision authority to officers. A frontier-lab-built system moving from three councils to fifteen, with a stated intention to reach every council in England within roughly fourteen months, is a procurement and governance event whatever the efficiency number turns out to be.

The number is worth reporting accurately for the same reason. If 58 per cent becomes the figure everyone cites for AI in UK planning, it should be cited as a departmental estimate with no published method, not as a finding.

A 58 per cent saving is a claim about a process. The government has not published the process it measured.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom’s own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

A planning department built an AI tool with Google DeepMind, tried it in three councils, and has now said it will go into fifteen for a year, with every council in England in its sights by the end of 2027. That is the news, and it is a real deployment rather than a pilot.

The number attached to it is 58 per cent: less time on key planning tasks, and routine householder applications halved from eight weeks to four. Every outlet will lead with that. Here is the thing to hold onto while they do. The 58 per cent is the department’s own figure, about its own tool, with no method published next to it — no sample, no baseline, no independent check. It might be exactly right. You cannot tell from the announcement, and neither can we.

Quieter, and arguably larger: from the day after the announcement, straightforward applications are decided by planning officers instead of elected committees. That changes who says yes, and it went out in the same press release as the AI story, underneath it.

If you are applying for a loft extension, this is mostly good news and slightly unknowable news. If you are the kind of person who likes to know whether a number is measured or asserted, this week gave you a clean example of the difference.
