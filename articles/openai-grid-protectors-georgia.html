OpenAI's Grid Protectors Pitch Rests on One Unbuilt Campus and a Household Saving That Moved From $102 to $180 a Year

By The Humans Are Fine newsroom · Published 25 November 2026

OpenAI launched a national grid programme on 2 November built on data centres that cut their power use at peak. Its only worked example is a Georgia campus whose power arrives in phases from 2028, whose flexibility is a contractual ceiling, and whose advertised household saving was revised up by three-quarters by the utility ten weeks before the announcement.

READ THE EVIDENCE FILE →

On 2 November 2026, OpenAI published a post launching the Grid Protectors Program, described in its opening sentence as "a national effort to support affordable, reliable electricity for families and businesses in the communities where we build." It is a statement of intent rather than a filing or a tariff. OpenAI describes "working with utilities to agree on how much demand can be reduced, how quickly and for how long," and that "the arrangements will differ by location, and we will share additional commitments as they are finalized."

The document contains exactly one worked example, and it is not new: it is a contract that cleared its regulatory step ten weeks earlier.

The example is a campus that does not exist yet. On 22 July 2026, OpenAI announced Project Camellia in Effingham County, Georgia, writing that "we are contracting with Georgia Power Company for 3.2 gigawatts of power which will be delivered in phases between 2028 and 2032," and that "we are still at the beginning of the development process." The November post carries the same status line: "Although the campus is still in development, we intend for Effingham to serve as a pilot for Grid Protectors, building on an agreement that already provides a model for future campuses."

Georgia Power's own release of 26 August 2026, published once the contract had been approved in the regulatory process, sets out the shape of the deal: "3,200 megawatts (MW) of new demand," under which "OpenAI will pay the full cost of the infrastructure needed to serve it and has committed up to 1,000 MW of flexible demand response, which enables Georgia Power to reduce energy delivered to the facility during periods of high demand and invest in fewer new generation resources to serve future growth."

Read together, the programme's founding principle — a computing campus that draws less when the grid is strained — rests on a flexibility ceiling of up to 1,000 megawatts, at most 31.25% of the 3,200 megawatts the campus is contracted to draw, at a site that has not been built.

What "up to 1,000 megawatts" does and does not say. The ceiling is real; the operating terms behind it are not published. Neither OpenAI's November post nor Georgia Power's August release states how many megawatts would actually be curtailed, for how long, on how much notice, how a reduction would be measured, or what happens if a campus declines to reduce. OpenAI's post offers only that "long-term contracts and financial assurances can protect other customers if a project is delayed, reduced or canceled," without describing the instruments. Its July announcement promised an accountability mechanism of a different kind — "an annual publicly available audit by an independent firm will be conducted and released" — attached to the commitments listed in that post, one of which is the pledge to reduce power before residential customers are affected. The audit is given no curtailment metric, no threshold and no reporting date.

The scale comparison in the post is OpenAI's own and carries no method: 1,000 megawatts is "equivalent to the average electricity demand of roughly 700,000 households. That's more households than Washington, D.C., and Boston combined." No baseline, year or conversion source is given, and a household-equivalent is not a bill.

The saving, and the revision. The money claim is where the documents become genuinely useful, because two of them can be set side by side.

OpenAI's post says its agreement "also contributes to Georgia Power's broader plan to lower costs for customers," and reports the utility's projection: "The utility projects approximately $950 million in annual customer savings beginning in 2029 from its portfolio of large-load agreements and expected growth, including Project Camellia. It estimates a benefit of at least $15 a month for a typical residential customer using 1,000 kilowatt-hours."

Georgia Power's 26 August release reports the same figures and adds the history OpenAI's post omits: "With the projects announced to date and projected future growth in the state, Georgia Power customers are expected to see savings of at least $15 per month beginning in 2029 for the typical residential customer using 1,000 kilowatt-hours per month, an increase from the company's December 2025 commitment of $102 per year to $180 per year."

The earlier commitment is documented. In a release dated 10 December 2025, Georgia Power described its stipulated agreement with the Public Interest Advocacy Staff of the Georgia Public Service Commission in these terms: "the agreement notes that, when the company files its next base rate case in 2028, it would do so in a manner that ensures incremental revenue from large-load customers of at least $556 million per year will put downward pressure on rates of at least $8.50 per month (or approximately $102 per year) for the typical residential customer using 1,000 kilowatt-hours per month."

Both figures are stated for one household using 1,000 kilowatt-hours a month, and Georgia Power's August release dates the higher figure to 2029. Its December 2025 release ties the $102 to the next base rate case, filed in 2028, without naming a start year. On that common basis the two are comparable. $102 a year is $8.50 a month; $180 a year is $15 a month. The utility is not contradicting itself. It is reporting that its own projection rose by roughly three-quarters in the eight and a half months between the two releases, and it says so in plain terms. "In December, we committed to $102 a year in savings for the typical family beginning in 2029," said chairman, president and CEO Kim Greene in the August release. "Today, that number is expected to be $180."

Whose money, and whose projection. Both documents present the saving as a forecast, in different words. OpenAI's post says the utility "projects" the $950 million and "estimates" the $15. Georgia Power's release says customers are "expected to see" it and that it is "the result of projected incremental revenue from new large-load customers." Georgia Power puts the total at "approximately $950 million per year, increasing customer benefits to $2.847 billion over the three-year period of 2029-2031." The $950 million and the $180 are projections rather than booked savings: they depend on campuses being built and drawing power, and on the load forecast holding.

The post's national framing is a projection of the same kind. It builds its case on the premise that "U.S. data center capacity projected to exceed 100 gigawatts by 2030" — a figure carried in the post on a hyperlink rather than named to a forecaster in the text, so it is attributed here to OpenAI and to no other organisation.

OpenAI does place a limit on its own argument: "Flexibility cannot replace investment in new energy supply and transmission." That sentence sits alongside the utility's own construction plans. In the December 2025 release Georgia Power said that, "if approved by the Georgia PSC", the stipulated agreement would let it "proceed with more than 3,600 MW of new combined cycle natural gas generation; more than 3,000 MW of new battery energy storage systems (BESS); 350 MW of BESS plus solar; and more than 2,800 MW of power purchase agreements." Flexibility at Effingham is presented as reducing how much new generation is needed, not as replacing the build.

What the regulator has said. The Georgia Public Service Commission's consumer page on data centres states: "These rules ensure NO residents or small businesses will see increases on their power bills due to data centers." The same page notes that "in July 2025, the PSC Commissioners instituted a Georgia Power rate freeze through the end of 2028."

Both are statements about rules on a consumer information page, not findings about the Effingham contract, and the timing matters: the freeze on base rates runs through the end of 2028, while every savings figure discussed here begins in 2029.

What would settle it. Three things are unpublished and would decide whether the programme is what it says. First, the curtailment terms for Effingham — megawatts available in practice, duration, notice, measurement and penalty. Second, the arithmetic behind $950 million and $180 a year, and whether the second revision follows the first. Third, whether the promised independent audit covers grid performance at all. Until then the accurate description is narrow: OpenAI has announced a national programme whose sole example is a 3,200-megawatt campus not yet built, with a flexibility ceiling of up to 1,000 megawatts and terms unwritten, and a household saving that the utility has already restated once — from $102 a year to $180 — before a single megawatt has been delivered.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom’s own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

Set beside Georgia Power's own releases, the November post reads less like a programme than a preview of one. The gap matters because everything a reader would need in order to judge the claim — how much power is actually curtailed, for how long, on what notice — sits in the space between an announcement and its operating terms, and the documents read here do not close it. What they do show is a utility restating its own household projection upward before any megawatt was delivered.

Watch three things: whether Effingham's curtailment terms are published, whether the arithmetic behind the savings appears, and whether the promised audit is given a grid metric. Until then, the announcement is the claim.
