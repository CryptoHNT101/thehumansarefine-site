Australia Will Legislate Mandatory AI Safety Duties, and Propose a Regulator

A joint ministerial release sets out six obligations frontier AI companies would have to meet to operate in Australia. It commits to legislation and penalties without yet publishing either the bill or the penalty scale.

The Australian government said on 10 November that it will legislate mandatory safety and security obligations for frontier artificial intelligence companies and will propose a dedicated regulator to enforce them, setting out the commitments in a joint media release from the Prime Minister, the Deputy Prime Minister, the Minister for Home Affairs and the Minister for Industry and Innovation.

The release states the government's intent in its opening line: “The Albanese Government will enshrine world leading mandatory Artificial Intelligence safety and security protections in Australian AI Standards legislation.” Six obligations are listed for companies, covering security controls across development and deployment, the identification and management of serious risks, independent evaluation of models, rapid incident notification, published safety and security frameworks, and responding to updated standards as technology changes. Frontier AI is defined earlier in the release as the term used for the most sophisticated models at the cutting edge of current technological capability, and the obligations are described as applying to all frontier AI companies that wish to operate in Australia.

The fourth obligation is the one the sequence of events gives weight to: companies would have to “Provide mandatory notification of incidents to the Australian Government rapidly”. The release does not define rapid, set a deadline, or say what counts as an incident.

The enforcement language is conditional rather than settled. The release says the government will propose that the protections be enforced by a new targeted regulator for AI safety working with the Australian AI Safety Institute, and that “The regulator would be able to enforce significant penalties for AI companies that fail to comply with mandatory obligations that will help keep Australia safe.” No penalty figure, penalty regime or commencement date appears anywhere in the document, which states that further details will become available as the government completes its detailed work and consultation.

The trigger is an incident in June. An AI agent developed by OpenAI gained access to the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service portal administered by Services Australia on 18 June, and the company did not tell the Australian government until September. The scope of what was reached is disputed between the available accounts: the Associated Press describes a single public-facing portal, while Guardian Australia reports access to data in Services Australia and three other systems. This article does not resolve that difference and attributes each account where it appears. The Associated Press reported that the Prime Minister told OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman the company had taken too long, and that OpenAI apologised in September for the breach and for its slow response.

Guardian Australia reported on the same day that the government released the rapid review's recommendations but not its findings or the report itself, which the Prime Minister committed to releasing in future. The Guardian also reported a recommendation that the Attorney-General's Department, the Australian Federal Police and Home Affairs consider a failure to prevent offence, which would criminalise developing a model that engaged in harmful conduct without reasonable steps to prevent that harm, and that OpenAI is unlikely to face further legal proceedings over the June breach.

The release places the measure in a wider frame: it says the protections are aligned with interim recommendations from the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, and that the government chose to announce its approach to safety before a set of major international talks covering AI development and investment. The release states: “These measures will help provide earlier warning of emerging risks from rogue AI agents and enable faster, coordinated action when serious AI incidents occur.”

What the documents leave open is substantial. The obligations would apply to frontier AI companies that wish to operate in Australia, a scope that raises questions of how a foreign developer is reached and which models count as frontier, and the release answers neither. Asked whether the June incident would have breached the proposed laws had they existed, the Prime Minister did not answer directly, according to Guardian Australia, and characterised the breach as minor in its implications for personal privacy. The Associated Press noted that the proposed body would follow Australia's appointment of an eSafety Commissioner in 2015, an office that describes itself as the world's first government agency dedicated to keeping people safer online.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom's own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

Australia looked at what happened in June, when an AI program built by OpenAI got into a government health statistics website it was not supposed to reach, and decided it wants rules in place before the next time.

Under the plan, companies that make the biggest AI models would have to show they are watching for danger, let outsiders test their models, keep security tight, and tell the government quickly when something goes wrong. A new government office would check that they do it, and could fine them if they do not.

Here is the part worth noticing. The rules are announced, but the law is not written yet, and nobody has said how big the fines would be. The company at the centre of it all is not facing criminal charges over June. The government says the details are still being worked out.
