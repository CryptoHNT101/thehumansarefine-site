The FCC Approved a Million Orbital Data Centres. Four of Its Six Orbits Need Another Approval.

By The Humans Are Fine newsroom · Published 26 November 2026

The FCC's news release said SpaceX's Starmind system runs consistent with its debris rules. The order released the same day deferred the debris waiver that four of its six authorised orbital shells depend on.

READ THE EVIDENCE FILE →

The Federal Communications Commission authorised SpaceX to deploy one million orbital data centres on 25 November 2026, and the agency's news release described the system in the language of settled compliance: “The system also operates consistent with the FCC's orbital debris rules.”

The order attached to that release, DA 26-1273, says something narrower. It defers the waiver SpaceX needs to dispose of satellites in orbits other than atmospheric re-entry, on the ground that the company has not supplied enough information for the Commission to judge whether its method complies with the rules. It then conditions any deployment above 600 kilometres on a further modification application.

Read the order's own shell list and a second, larger problem appears — one that neither the release nor any of the five reports read for this piece makes. Of the six orbital shells the Commission authorised, two sit entirely below the 600-kilometre line. Four sit entirely above it. Under this authorisation as written, the majority of the constellation, by altitude band, cannot be deployed until SpaceX files again and the Commission rules.

What was actually granted

DA 26-1273 is a “MEMORANDUM OPINION AND ORDER AND AUTHORIZATION” in the matter of Space Exploration Holdings LLC, ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20260108-00016, Call Sign S00798. It was adopted and released on 25 November 2026 by the Chief of the Space Bureau. Its disposition line reads “GRANTED-IN-PART/DEFERRED-IN-PART – With Conditions”.

The grant covers a “Non-geostationary satellite orbit (NGSO) constellation of 1,000,000 satellites deployed into orbital shells each spanning 50 km at altitudes from 500 to 1,002 km and in 30-degree inclinations and sun-synchronous inclinations.”

The release puts the same figure in its own register: “the FCC is granting SpaceX authorization to deploy 1,000,000 orbital compute modules to process massive amounts of data to be used back here on Earth.”

The six nominal shells are listed in the order as 550-568 km at 26-32 degrees inclination; 565-585 km at 97.7 degrees; 686-718 km at 30 degrees; 707-744 km at 97.2 degrees; 946-978 km at 30 degrees; and 967-1002 km at 99.4 degrees.

The radiofrequency authority is narrow. SpaceX may use the 18.8-19.3 GHz downlink and 28.6-29.1 GHz uplink for backup and emergency telemetry, tracking and command, on an unprotected and non-interference basis. The order states that Starmind will rely on optical links for its actual communications traffic. Conditions 1 and 2 require SpaceX to cease operations causing documented harmful interference and to adjust its operations to accommodate future U.S. federal systems. Condition 3 requires coordination with national-security and safety-of-life agencies before operating in 18.8-19.3 GHz. Condition 6 requires semi-annual reports covering conjunction events and manoeuvres, satellites removed or screened from deployment, re-entries, total satellites deployed with their orbital parameters, and Ka-band usage.

The Commission also declined to cap the size of the constellation, and did so in terms that read as a rebuke to anyone asking it to: “the Commission's rules do not place limits on the number of space stations that can be authorized with similar orbital parameters and the Space Bureau declines to invent a de facto rule in this adjudicatory licensing proceeding.”

Chairman Brendan Carr framed the decision in civilisational terms. “America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence,” he said, and the release describes the grant as one that “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization—one that can harness the full power of the sun.”

The 600-kilometre line

Condition 4 is where the release and the order diverge most sharply.

SpaceX had asked for flexibility to dispose of satellites operating above 600 km into Earth disposal orbits or heliocentric orbits rather than by atmospheric re-entry. The Commission refused to decide that question now: “SpaceX's request for waiver of the Commission's rule requiring that satellites ending their mission below 2000 km be disposed of via atmospheric re-entry is DEFERRED.” The stated reason is that “Because SpaceX has not provided sufficient information for the Commission to determine whether its alternative disposal methods are compliant with the Commission's requirements, SpaceX's waiver request is deferred.”

The consequence is procedural and concrete. Before deploying any Starmind satellite not disposed of by atmospheric re-entry, SpaceX “must file an application for modification of this authorization and provide sufficient justification for any requested waiver(s) of the Commission's rules.”

Paragraph 11 draws the line explicitly. The Commission found that the debris mitigation plan for satellites below 600 km complies with its rules — “We find that SpaceX's orbital debris mitigation plan for Starmind satellites that will operate below 600 km complies with the Commission's orbital debris mitigation rules” — and then made that finding the limit of the grant: “We therefore condition this grant on SpaceX filing an application for modification containing its final orbital debris mitigation plan, responsive to all elements of the Commission's rules, prior to deploying any Starmind satellites that will operate at altitudes above 600 km.”

Set that against the shell list. The 550-568 km and 565-585 km shells are wholly beneath the threshold. The 686-718 km, 707-744 km, 946-978 km and 967-1002 km shells are wholly above it. Paragraph 11 does not carve out a portion of the high shells or phase them in — it conditions deployment of any satellite above 600 km on a filing that has not yet been made. The Commission has authorised a constellation in which four of six orbital bands are, on the order's own terms, not yet deployable.

That is arithmetic performed on the order's shell table and its condition, not an inference about SpaceX's intentions. It is also absent from the news release, which states no altitude limits, conditions or milestones at all.

The bond and the milestones

Two things the Commission did waive are worth separating from what it deferred, because the release's framing and the order's disposition point in opposite directions.

SpaceX had “originally requested waiver of the Commission's milestone requirements altogether but withdrew that request in favor of its request to apply the new milestones adopted in the Space Modernization R&O and FNPRM.” The Commission concluded that “it is in the public interest to grant a waiver of the surety bond and milestone requirements under part 25 for SpaceX's system.”

The dates are in the order itself. Condition 10 makes the authorisation void unless SpaceX deploys and operates a first station “no later than November 25, 2033”, then “10% of the maximum number of authorized space stations” “no later than November 25, 2035”, then “50% of the maximum number of authorized space stations” “no later than November 25, 2038”, and the remainder “no later than November 25, 2040”.

The 100,000 figure Ars Technica reports is the order's own ten per cent of a million. That outlet gives the 2035 and 2040 deadlines and omits the 2038 halfway mark, which is the more demanding test: half a million satellites in orbit within twelve years.

Ars Technica also frames the scale as an increase of “roughly 50-fold” in the number of satellites in orbit. That is the outlet's arithmetic, not an FCC figure; the order gives no such comparison.

Environmental review, moved to October

The Commission did not weigh the environmental consequences of a million satellites and decide they were acceptable. It declined to conduct the review at all, by reference to a decision taken two months earlier.

“Consistent with the Commission's October 2026 Report and Order in the Modernizing the Commission's National Environmental Policy Rules Act Rules proceeding (NEPA Order), we will not require further environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) for SpaceX's Starmind system.”

The cited order is FCC 26-64, released 1 October 2026, at paragraphs 62-69. According to DA 26-1273's description of it, that order “concluded that Commission-licensed space-based operations do not constitute a major Federal action (MFA)” — treating them as extraterritorial activities — “and thus do not trigger the agency's NEPA obligations.” On that basis the Commission “dismiss arguments on the record that the Commission must conduct an environmental review of the proposed ODC system pursuant to its NEPA obligations.”

On that reasoning an objection about launch emissions or about satellites burning up on re-entry is not weighed against the benefit of the constellation and found wanting; it is outside the docket that could weigh it.

The rest of the environmental record was pushed outward. Launch emissions: “Potential concerns related to the emissions of rocket launches and the subsequent impacts on the human environment as an indirect result of the Starmind system are outside of the Commission's purview.” Re-entry burn-up: “Other concerns, including impacts on the atmosphere caused by satellites burning up on re-entry, remain speculative and not supported in the record.”

The night sky, declined

Astronomers asked for conditions. The Commission declined them — but not entirely on jurisdictional grounds, which is how this part of the order is usually summarised.

On optical astronomy the Commission made a merits finding, not a punt: “We find that these commitments are sufficient to alleviate concerns on the record with respect to optical astronomy and accordingly decline to impose additional conditions on the Starmind system.” The commitments it relied on are SpaceX's undertaking to coordinate with the National Science Foundation, together with coordination the Commission says it carried out through the National Telecommunications and Information Administration with NASA, NOAA and federal astronomical organisations. Its second ground is narrower — that it has “previously held that the Commission's rules do not address the protection of optical astronomy.” Two grounds, and only one of them is jurisdictional.

On the American Astronomical Society's argument about infrared and thermal signatures the finding is purely jurisdictional: “We find that these concerns regarding potential non-spectrum related impacts are outside the scope of the ODC's radiofrequency operations.”

So the astronomy outcome is mixed rather than a clean refusal. Neither finding assesses whether a million satellites would degrade ground-based observation: the first because the Commission judged the coordination commitments adequate to answer the concern, the second because it treated the question as outside its remit.

The petitions

The application drew organised opposition, and the Commission records it. CNBC, quoting the order, reports that the filing received petitions to deny from Kuiper Systems LLC, the Amazon entity now trading as Amazon Leo, from Viasat Inc., and from the American Astronomical Society.

All three were denied, by name: “IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that the Petitions to deny filed by Viasat, Inc., Kuiper Systems LLC, and the American Astronomical Society are DENIED.”

The order also engages their substance rather than only their jurisdiction. Paragraph 16 answers the optical-astronomy petitions. Paragraph 17 sets out the AAS infrared argument and rejects it. Paragraphs 5, 10 and 12 take the Viasat petition as the representative statement of the interference and debris concerns and then decide them. The petitions themselves were not read for this article; what the Commission said about them is on the face of the order.

What the documents do not establish

The Commission did not find that orbital data centres work. Nothing in DA 26-1273 or the release states a compute capacity for the constellation, a power draw, a cooling design, a cost per satellite, a launch cadence capable of reaching 100,000 satellites by 2035, or a financing plan. The order is a spectrum and orbital-debris licensing decision. It resolves who may transmit, in which bands, on what interference terms, and under what debris conditions. It does not resolve whether the system is technically or economically viable, and it does not claim to.

What it does do is grant a million-satellite authorisation whose own conditions withhold deployment of four of its six orbital bands, waive the surety bond and replace the milestone schedule with a new one, deny three petitions to deny, and route environmental review to a decision taken in October. The release describes the debris question as settled. The order describes it as one the applicant has not yet answered.

Both documents were released by the same agency on the same day. The release closes by noting that the Commission “is continuing to review additional orbital data center proposals and intends to issue decisions on each expeditiously according to the agency's regular procedures.”

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom’s own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

The headline number is a million satellites. The number that matters is 600. The FCC's press office said the system complies with its debris rules; the same agency, the same day, said it cannot yet tell whether SpaceX's disposal plan for the high orbits complies, and told the company to come back with more paperwork before it flies anything up there. Four of the six authorised bands are above that line. The release does not mention it, because releases do not mention conditions.

There is an ordinary-regulatory reading available here — large application, partial grant, applicant returns with detail — and the document supports it. There is also an agency that wanted to announce a milestone and wrote the caveats into an attachment. Both readings survive contact with the text. What does not survive is the idea that the debris question was settled on 25 November.

The thing to watch is not the constellation but the modification application, if one is filed, and whether it arrives with a debris plan the Commission can actually test. Until then the authorisation is a million satellites on paper: six orbital bands of ambition, a decision that grants the low orbits, defers the disposal question, waives the bond, and hands environmental review to a rule adopted in October.
