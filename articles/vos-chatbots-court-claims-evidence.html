CLAIMS

C01 — Vos is retiring as Master of the Rolls, the most senior civil judge in England

Source (S1): “In a few days, judge Geoffrey Vos will retire as Master of the Rolls, the most senior position in England’s civil justice system.” Status: SUPPORTED (single source, WIRED’s own characterisation).

C02 — He uses Copilot daily, but not to write judgments

Source (S1): “I don’t use it for writing judgments, but I use it for finding things out, navigating large quantities of material, and checking things.” / “I do that every day.” Status: SUPPORTED, direct quotation.

C03 — He visited a Shenzhen court and came back impressed

Source (S1): “recently, he visited a court in Shenzhen, China, where civil disputes are already being decided by judges with heavy assistance from AI. Impressed by what he saw, Vos believes that courts across the globe should consider following.” Status: SUPPORTED as WIRED’s reporting of Vos’s view. NOT independently verified.

C04 — Current English guidance confines judicial AI use to administrative tasks

Source (S1, S3): “Under the latest guidance, judges in England are advised to confine their use of AI to administrative tasks.” Status: ATTRIBUTED TO S1. The guidance itself was not retrieved. The article words this as “the current guidance in England, which confines judicial use of AI to administrative tasks” — acceptable only because the sentence’s function is to establish that Vos is pushing against a restriction; if the restriction is misdescribed, that contrast weakens. Flagged for the reviewer.

C05 — He names family and complex commercial cases as requiring human judgment

Source (S1): “I think that custody and family cases are one of the areas where essentially human judgment is expected, even by the younger generation. In complex commercial cases, I think that’s also the case.” Status: SUPPORTED. Note the hedge “I think” is present in the source; the article paraphrases as “he named two categories that should stay human,” which is faithful but loses the hedge. Minor.

C06 — He names personal injury and low-value professional negligence as AI-suitable

Source (S1): “he believes that personal injury claims, low-value professional negligence cases, and the like could easily be adjudicated by AI.” Status: SUPPORTED. This is WIRED’s paraphrase of Vos, not a direct quote from him, and the article treats it as his view — which is what the source says it is.

C07 — “What most people desperately want in civil disputes is resolution”

Source (S1): direct quotation, with “The question is, where is the line to be drawn?” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim.

C08 — He declines to draw the line himself, deferring to society and legislation

Source (S1): “I don’t think judges are yet in the position to say. It’s a question for society, politicians, and legislation to resolve.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim.

C09 — He conditions algorithmic decision-making on consent or legislation

Source (S1): “It will only be possible to ask people to accept algorithmic decision-making if they consent to the process in a particular case, or if society decides through legislation.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim.

C10 — County court case starts rising 10% a year, attributed to chatbots

Source (S1): “We see a 10 percent rise in the number of county court cases started every year — and it looks as if this may increase. That’s because everybody who has a legal problem now puts it into Gemini, ChatGPT, or Claude.” Status: QUOTED ACCURATELY, FIGURE UNCORROBORATED. The causal claim is Vos’s, offered without data. The article says so explicitly and does not adopt it.

C11 — He concedes the data on claim quality does not exist

Source (S1): “We don’t have the data.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim. This is the hinge of the article’s argument and it is a direct quotation, not an inference.

C12 — He argues a rise in claims reflects wider access to justice

Source (S1): “One of the problems with justice in the past is that it’s only been available to rich people. If people have a problem sufficient to bother ChatGPT, they probably genuinely think they have a claim... People are not generally time wasters.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim (elision marked in the article as “...”, inside a block quote).

C13 — Lawyers have been caught citing hallucinated case law; one lost a licence

Source (S1): “Multiple lawyers have been caught citing case law hallucinated by AI, and in a first for the UK, somebody recently lost their license to practice in part because they misused the technology.” Status: ATTRIBUTED TO WIRED, not independently verified. The article names WIRED as the source of both facts in its closing note.

C14 — On fabricated evidence: no difference “in scale or qualitatively”

Source (S1): “There’s absolutely no difference — in scale or qualitatively — in somebody producing a false image created by AI.” / “We always had to decide whether wills were forged, because people used to do that.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim. The article’s objection to “in scale” is ANALYSIS, labelled as such (“That ’in scale’ is doing a lot of unexamined work”) and supported by reasoning about cost and volume, not by a contradicting source. It is presented as the reporter’s argument.

C15 — Perjury deterrent

Source (S1): “they will go to prison, just as if they’d forged a will.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim.

C16 — Explainability: “it’s about outcomes... judge the plausibility”

Source (S1): “I don’t think it’s about reasoning; it’s about outcomes. The AI can produce very plausible reasoning. You can’t see how it produced that reasoning, but you can see the reasoning it produced, and you can judge the plausibility.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim. The article’s response — that “plausibility is a weaker test than correctness” — is ANALYSIS, labelled as such.

C17 — Human judges will not be done away with

Source (S1): “I don’t think you’ll ever do away with human judges. There’s something almost biblical about the human judge; it’s in the human psyche to want to be judged by their fellow man. But that does not mean that in some situations people wouldn’t rather have a quick outcome — even if it’s not a human outcome.” Status: SUPPORTED, verbatim.

C18 — He describes his own stake as philosophical

Source (S1): “Given his impending retirement, the debate for Vos is largely a philosophical one.” Status: SUPPORTED. Note: this is WIRED’s framing sentence, not Vos’s own words. The article writes “he says as much, describing the debate in his own case as ’largely a philosophical one’” — which attributes to Vos a phrase that is the journalist’s. DEFECT. Must be reworded to attribute the characterisation to WIRED, or dropped.

C19 — Legora figures: 90% faster turnaround, 9.5 hours per lawyer per week, 95%+ NDA reduction, $20M+ saved

Source (S2): “90% faster contract turnaround for a Fortune 100 customer”; “9.5 hours returned per lawyer, per week”; “95%+ reduction in Legora legal-team time spent reviewing NDAs”; “$20M+ saved on external litigation and diligence for the same customer.” Status: SUPPORTED AS PUBLISHED, AND SELF-REPORTED. OpenAI describes the customer as unnamed (“one Fortune 100 customer”). No period is given for the $20M figure. The article states these are claims and names the customer as unnamed; it does not present them as verified measurements.

C20 — Legora is Stockholm-founded, 2023, and OpenAI is “a key model provider”

Source (S2): “Founded in Stockholm in 2023”; “OpenAI is a key model provider for Legora”. Status: SUPPORTED. The article says “Stockholm-founded legal platform” — accurate. It does not describe OpenAI as exclusive, because the source says “a key model provider.”

C21 — The two documents never reference each other

Source: absence across the full text of both S1 and S2. Status: SUPPORTED as an absence, tested against full text of both. Neither document names the other or its subject.

Residual limitations

1. Single-subject interview. The article rests on one person’s edited remarks plus one vendor page. No opposing interviewee, no court statistics, no guidance document. 2. The 10% figure is uncorroborated. It is the article’s most quotable number and its weakest link. The article attributes it to Vos and says the underlying statistics were not obtained. 3. The licence-loss claim is second-hand from WIRED and unverified. 4. C18 is a genuine attribution defect and is fixed below before this file is considered closed. 5. The article’s argument that volume undermines the “no difference in scale” claim is reasoning, not evidence. It is marked as analysis in the text and must stay marked.

NEW CLAIMS

C24 — Vos made substantially the same argument in October 2025, including the personal-injury example

Source (S4): “The answer is not obvious, because nobody can really tell me why AI should not be used to assess, for example, personal injury damages by reference to the numerous authorities found in the textbooks. That task would take an AI a couple of minutes, whilst the wait for a judicial hearing and determination might be more like two years.” Status: SUPPORTED, direct quotation from a published speech. The article draws the correct inference: the interview is a restatement on the eve of retirement, not a late conversion.

C25 — Vos has himself raised the Article 6 objection, and the interview does not

Source (S4): “It is whether a machine-made decision can ever be properly regarded as having been made by an 'independent and impartial tribunal established by law' for the purposes of article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms.” Also S4: the threefold answer — decisions are “the last resort”; machines “will arguably never be able completely satisfactorily to mimic a human’s emotion, idiosyncracy, empathy and insight”; and a machine decision is “generated from the state of intelligence at a given point in time, without the application of developing human thought”. Status: SUPPORTED. This is the most important correction in the rewrite. The earlier draft presented his position without the strongest objection to it, which he had already articulated himself, publicly, a year earlier. Omitting it flattered neither side.

C26 — The published volume is real and close to his figure

Source (S5): “Compared to the same period in 2025, County Court claims from April to June 2026 were up 11% to 571,000.” Source (S1): “We see a 10 percent rise in the number of county court cases started every year”. Status: SUPPORTED as two separate published figures. The article says his figure is close to the published one. It does not say the two series are identical, because “every year” and a quarterly year-on-year comparison are not the same measure.

C27 — The Ministry of Justice attributes the rise to parking-debt recovery, not to chatbots

Source (S5): “In the most recent quarter, total claims were up 11% compared to the same period in 2025 (from 514,000 to 571,000), likely driven by a rise in claims to recover monies in relation to parking on private land.” Source (S5): “Of these, 495,000 (87%) were money claims”, with money claims up 13 percent. Source (S5): “The increase in the volume of money claims is mostly driven by claims valued under £500.” Status: SUPPORTED. The word “likely” is the Ministry’s own and the article preserves it — the bulletin does not establish causation either. The article says explicitly that both explanations are inferences.

C28 — The category Vos nominates for automation is contracting

Source (S5): “Damages claims ... were down 4% to 26,000, driven by a decrease in personal injury claims and other damages claims (both down 4%) to 11,000 and 15,000 respectively compared to the same quarter in 2025.” Status: SUPPORTED. The article uses this to establish a composition problem and states plainly that it “does not refute him” — the volume rise is real, it simply is not in the category under debate.

C29 — The concession is about merit, not about causation

Source (S1): the question put to Vos was “What proportion of these claims are based on sound legal principles, though? How often are people led by a chatbot to mistakenly believe they have a claim?” — answered “We don’t have the data.” Status: SUPPORTED. The earlier draft used this concession to carry the causal argument. That was a scoping error and is corrected: the article now states the answer is about merit, reports it as being about merit, and says it is not a concession on causation.

C30 — The hedges in the interview are real and are quoted

Source (S1): “I don’t think you’ll ever do away with human judges.” / “With AI, we have to be careful; it may be less easy for people to accept an adverse machine-driven decision.” / “When you explore their problem in detail, you might find it is actually a bit different, but it may still be a legal problem and may still be reasonable for them to have wanted to bring it to attention.” Status: SUPPORTED. The earlier draft cut the third of these and under-used the other two.

Scope note, recorded because it matters. The worker was given a superseded draft — the one headed “Wants AI Deciding Cases. He Concedes Nobody Has the Data.” The article was repaired after that draft was taken. So its three headline findings describe defects that had already been fixed before it ran. That is independent convergence of a reviewer on repairs I had made, which is useful confirmation but not new work. I re-verified each of its assertions against the archive rather than accepting them.

Its three findings, checked

1. “The concession was about claim merit, not causation.” Correct, and already in the article: “That answer is about merit — whether the claims are sound — and it should be reported as being about merit. It is not a concession that the causal claim is unsupported.” The current headline no longer rests on the concession; it rests on the MoJ attribution. 2. “The MoJ publishes the volume series the piece says nobody has.” Correct, and already used. The article now leads on the bulletin’s own sentence — “likely driven by a rise in claims to recover monies in relation to parking on private land” — and on damages claims falling 4% to 26,000 with personal injury down to 11,000. 3. “Omission of his published Article 6 and finality objections.” Correct, and already repaired. The 15 October 2025 speech is now quoted in the article, including the Article 6 passage verbatim and the “last resort” objection.

What it did not catch, and I did — a fidelity defect the harness cannot see

The article said Vos told WIRED he uses Copilot “all the time”. In the WIRED text the phrase “all the time” occurs once, and it is the magazine’s narration, not Vos’s speech: “Vos tells WIRED he uses Microsoft Copilot all the time to locate far-flung documents and summarize large bodies of text.”

His own words, earlier in the same interview: “I don’t use it for writing judgments, but I use it for finding things out, navigating large quantities of material, and checking things.” And “Copilot finds it in seconds. I do that every day.”

So “every day” is genuinely quoted speech and “all the time” is not. The quote harness could never have flagged this: the string exists in the corpus, so it resolves. The defect is attribution, not existence — which is exactly the class of error a string-matching test cannot reach, and the reason a human read still sits in the chain.

Fixed. The sentence now distinguishes the two: WIRED reports he uses Copilot “all the time”; Vos’s own account is that he uses it “for finding things out, navigating large quantities of material, and checking things”, and that the search pattern is one he follows “every day”.

One addition, from the bulletin, fully sourced

The bulletin publishes a sharper composition figure than the article was using. Alongside “mostly driven by claims valued under £500”, it states: “These were up 21% to 245,000 claims in the period April to June 2026.” The bulletin also states that “Almost all (over 99%) money claims are processed and issued at the Civil National Business Centre (CNBC)”, recording 494,000 such claims there in the quarter — which is what supports the article’s CNBC passage, quoted verbatim from that document.

Corrected at final review. The article originally described the caseload, unquoted, as “bulk recovery of small money debts ... processed overwhelmingly through the Civil National Business Centre”, and the standfirst and homepage summary both used “bulk claims”. “Bulk” is a characterisation the bulletin does not use, so it was removed from all three places; the CNBC processing claim was retained but re-sourced as the verbatim quotation above. No quoted figure changed.

Added: sub-£500 claims “were up 21% to 245,000” — growing faster than money claims as a whole. This makes the counter-claim concrete rather than categorical, and it is the bulletin’s arithmetic, not mine.
