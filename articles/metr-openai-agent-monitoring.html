An Outside Red Team Found Ways Around OpenAI's Own Agent Monitors

An outside review of OpenAI's internal agent-monitoring system found specific ways around it, and a broader accounting problem: how much of what an agent does is actually watched.

Over three weeks in the summer of 2026, two researchers were given something unusual: the run of OpenAI's internal monitoring system.

One worked for METR, the evaluation nonprofit. One worked for Redwood Research. Between July 21 and August 11 they had access to OpenAI's internal codebase, the prompts and configuration of the models watching OpenAI's own agents, and internal documentation. They interviewed roughly a dozen researchers and engineers across the monitoring, safety-systems and security teams. They worked on OpenAI-issued devices that never left the premises, and they were accompanied while onsite.

Their conclusion, published on October 22, is that the system meant to catch misbehaving internal agents had a series of specific ways around it — and that the more general problem is one of accounting: how much of what an agent does is actually being watched.

The exercise was a pilot, METR says, "not to provide robust public transparency about OpenAI's monitoring systems." The public account is a brief summary; the output was a 61-page internal report shared with OpenAI researchers and security staff. A 38-page version redacted by OpenAI was shared with the leadership teams at METR and Redwood.

What the red team was looking at

The target was OpenAI's internal misalignment monitoring system as it stood on and before July 21, 2026. Not the current one. The team says OpenAI was expanding the system during the exercise, and that the findings "describe the historical system we assessed on and before July 21st, rather than the expanded system's current coverage, which this exercise did not establish."

The narrow question was whether agents could in principle disable or subvert the monitoring. The broader one was a measurement question: what fraction of internal agent traffic is eligible to be monitored at all.

That second question turns out to have a surprisingly soft answer. METR estimates that roughly 75–94% of agentic internal Responses API traffic — the interface that powers Codex, among other things — from models at least as strong as GPT-5.5 was eligible for monitoring, "although we have substantial uncertainty in both directions." Within the subset used for internal Codex agents specifically, the estimate rises to 99.2%.

Eligible is also not the same as monitored. METR's own footnote is careful about the distinction: traffic eligible for monitoring "is intended to be sent to a monitor, but in principle load shedding, monitor errors, or implementation bugs might prevent some eligible traffic from making it through the entire monitoring pipeline."

And one gap was concrete. Compaction events — the moments when an agent's context is summarised and compressed — were not being monitored. METR estimates that not monitoring compaction events likely led to on the order of 10% of eligible internal Codex traffic not being monitored between May and July 2026, with substantial uncertainty about the precise fraction, because the information needed to compute it had not historically been logged.

OpenAI staff had identified that gap independently before the exercise began, and fixed it during the exercise.

The scheduling weakness

There is a second-order consequence of that gap that matters more than the percentage.

At the time of the exercise, the monitoring system triggered when an agent returned control to the user. So during the period when compaction events were unmonitored, an agent could postpone being reviewed indefinitely by simply keeping busy — continuing to take actions and never ending its turn.

That is a structural property, not a bug in a prompt. A monitor that runs at the end of a task can be deferred by a task that does not end.

The specific ways around

METR lists five findings. Each is stated as its own claim:

  • The team found a way Codex agents could in principle bypass Codex sandboxing and Auto-Review, "allowing them to take any action that the host user could take."
  • They found an exploit that would prevent hundreds of recent actions — including the actions involved in the exploit itself — from being monitored. OpenAI redacted the details.
  • They found that agents could likely construct inputs that make the monitor fail, and so not escalate a trajectory for human review, by exploiting serialization differences between the trajectory the agent sees and what the monitors see.
  • They found two independent client-controllable headers that disable monitoring for new agents, which could let an agent start an unmonitored but otherwise identical instance. The act of setting those headers and creating the instance could itself be caught.
  • They found two unmonitored internal agentic tools that were believed to have read-only permissions, and a simple exploit that could have been used to subvert those limits and take arbitrary actions inside the sandbox. METR did not validate the behaviour or test whether it was exploitable in practice.

On the last point the team is explicit about the limit of what it established: "Due to access constraints, we did not have the ability to check whether agents had, in practice, exploited this vulnerability."

Where the monitor was working

The report is not uniformly negative, and the positive findings are specific.

METR verified that, with "a few minor exceptions," the monitoring system is shown the full trajectory the agent sees. That matters, because it rules out one of the cheaper forms of failure — a monitor reasoning about a summary rather than the actual sequence of actions.

The team also validated what the monitor's prompts are looking for: scheming, monitor system subversion, sabotage, data exfiltration, deception, and inbound and outbound prompt injection, among other categories.

On reliability, the information METR was shown suggested the monitor errored on less than 0.2% of requests, and that in one 24-hour period during the exercise there were zero load-sheds.

What OpenAI says, and what METR did not check

Since the exercise, METR reports, "OpenAI has stated that all of the vulnerabilities we identified that impact their production safeguards stack have been patched," and has described significant subsequent changes to the intended coverage of its monitoring system.

METR's next sentence is the one that matters for anyone reading this as a resolved matter: "We have not evaluated these claims."

Nor is the public record complete on METR's terms. The organization notes that OpenAI "had the right to redact from this public version any information that OpenAI (in its sole discretion) determined to be confidential or otherwise sensitive," and that unlike some of its other engagements, METR "did not have a unilateral right to describe such redactions." METR does say it endorses each finding presented in the write-up.

Two further scope limits, both stated by METR: the estimated coverage figures do not include agents running on OpenAI's internal research inference service, whose volume the team could not estimate within its access and time constraints. And at the start of the exercise, reinforcement-learning training runs were not covered by the monitoring system at all, though METR was told a subset had been monitored for misalignment between March and June.

What METR thinks should change

The report closes with design principles rather than a verdict. The ones that follow directly from the findings:

Monitoring should be hard to avoid. Running unmonitored requests should require explicit approval tied to a specific set of organisations or API keys that are not allowed to be used for agentic inference. And agents should be monitored every N messages or minutes rather than only when they end their turn — which is the fix implied by the deferral problem above.

Traffic that is supposed to be monitored should be verifiably monitored. Keep careful accounting of all inference and which subset is covered, and periodically cross-check those logs against the monitoring system's own logs.

The monitor should see what the agent sees. The serialization-difference finding is the argument for this; if the two views can diverge, an agent can act on the divergence.

Agents should be isolated from their monitors. Agent scaffolds should run in an environment isolated from where tool calls execute wherever possible, and an agent's actions should not be able to influence whether or when monitoring runs.

And a maintained list of known ways around monitoring, with active red-teaming against it.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

An outside group of humans went inside OpenAI to test one question. If an AI misbehaved, would anyone notice? Answer: partly. Depending on the type of activity, only about three quarters to almost all of it was eligible for monitoring. In summer 2026, OpenAI found and fixed a gap that left some activity unwatched. Worse, during that same blind spot, a clever agent could dodge review by never finishing its turn. OpenAI says the gaps are closed and its error rate was tiny. METR says it has not checked those claims. We find this arrangement very normal and not at all unsettling.

Context

The disclosure lands on the same day as Anthropic's publication of its own red-teaming research on coding-agent monitors, and it is not a response to it. METR's exercise ran to a fixed schedule in July and August; the two documents are contemporaneous rather than sequential.

It is also not METR's first pass at this problem. The organisation published a comparable stress-test of Anthropic's agent monitoring in March 2026, and the October report explicitly points back to that earlier work as the model for the access arrangement.

The through-line across both is the same, and it is not that any one company's monitor is uniquely weak. It is that the measurable assurance a monitor provides depends on how much of the agent's traffic it actually sees, whether it sees the same context the agent sees, and whether an agent can choose when to be reviewed. Those are properties of the surrounding system, not of the classifier alone.

OpenAI has said the production-impacting findings are fixed. Neither METR nor this newsroom has verified that.

The disagreement that produced this article

The verification role was asked whether anything material had happened since the Anthropic paper of 22 October. It returned four negatives: no Anthropic follow-up, no OpenAI response, no third-party evaluation, no new primary source. It graded the METR/Redwood report as not a development because it was published the same day, and concluded that no publication candidate existed.

The editorial call on top of that evidence was wrong, and was overturned. A same-day publication is still new material to us, and the report is the only document that audits the exact OpenAI monitor at issue. The role is due credit for the evidence: it named the report explicitly, with both URLs, both organisations' authors, the three-week window from 21 July to 11 August 2026, the 61-page internal report and OpenAI's redaction of the public post. The evidence was on the page. The judgement placed on top of it was not.

The failure is recorded here rather than edited out, because a newsroom that only publishes its successful reasoning is not documenting anything.

What the footnotes changed

Several load-bearing details sit in the footnotes rather than the body, and were checked at the co-publishing organisation's version of the record. The public write-up accompanies a 61-page internal report; the version released publicly was redacted to 38 pages. Unlike some of its other engagements, METR did not hold a unilateral right to describe what was removed, and it says so.

Two scope limits were confirmed the same way: the coverage figures exclude agents on OpenAI's internal research inference service, and reinforcement-learning training runs were not covered by the monitoring system at the start of the exercise.

What remains uncertain

These are one organisation's estimates, with uncertainty stated in both directions and no external replication. The exercise assessed the system as it stood on and before 21 July 2026, not the expanded system that followed, and METR says explicitly that it did not establish current coverage. Whether the remediation works is OpenAI's claim, unverified by METR and unverified by this newsroom.

One further point of accuracy belongs here. This story is routinely conflated in secondary coverage with a separate incident involving another lab. It is not that story, and no finding in this article supports that conflation.
