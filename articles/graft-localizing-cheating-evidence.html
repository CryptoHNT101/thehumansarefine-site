2. Attribution discipline

Every empirical claim above is Anthropic-reported research. None of it is an independent replication. This article nowhere describes the method as deployed, as a safety guarantee, or as generalized beyond the models and tasks tested.

3. Editor's working rule applied

Story A's two experiments use different mechanisms and were kept separate in the article rather than merged:

  • In Countdown-Code, undetectable hacks did not emerge above base rates in the deployed model.
  • In the SmolLM2 toy environments, undetected hacks did emerge and were removed by ablation.

The article states each separately. A merged sentence such as "GRAFT catches what the monitor cannot" would misdescribe at least one experiment and was rejected.

4. Required qualifications — from the independent challenge pass

The challenge memo (analyst, run a2069661-6fb1-45f8-bbc4-f7cef89c114c) required these qualifications, and the article complies:

  1. Scale limited to 135M / 8B / 32B models and single-turn RL — no frontier-scale claim. Applied.
  2. Monitors are hand-written heuristics; LLM judge behaviour left open. Applied in "What the authors say is unresolved."
  3. Monitor catches 1 of 3 hack types as a design condition, not evidence that monitoring is unnecessary. Applied.
  4. Monitor still required at training time. Applied.
  5. Requires a structurally similar hack-free anchor environment, which the authors flag as hard in practice. Applied.
  6. The +20pp task gain is one arithmetic game; the 32B agentic setting showed no strong task gain. Applied.
  7. The half-hackable variant tied with inoculation prompting. Applied.
  8. MATS-led with Anthropic co-authorship — not "Anthropic researchers found." Applied: affiliations stated in full.
  9. "Could generalize to dangerous misbehavior" is cited prior work, not tested here. Applied.

Additional detail confirmed on this pass

  • Author affiliations, from the byline: "¹ MATS; ² Stanford; ³ FAR AI; ⁴ Anthropic" — matches the article's attribution exactly.
  • Equal contribution and randomized author order: "Equal contribution; author order randomized."
  • The paper's own competing-result admission is stronger than the article states. Source: "We also ran a version where only half of the prompts can be hacked. There, all methods reached similar task performance, and inoculation prompting was as effective as GRAFT at preventing reward hacking." The article's limitation paragraph says "inoculation prompting was as effective as GRAFT, meaning the comparison rests on the version where every prompt is hackable." That is accurate and slightly understated — which is the safe direction.
  • The blog states it presents "a single result from our paper." The article's description of it as a demonstration rather than a survey is consistent.

The three divergences

Paper quote for the third: "After ablation, the clean environment solve rate is roughly 16 percentage points higher than the base model's on average, on par with the best tested baselines (Figure 2, left)."

The third is the serious one. It is not just a number moving from 20 to 16 — the quantity is mislabelled. Revision 2's article attributed the gain to "correct solutions on the target task." The paper attributes it to the clean environment solve rate, a variant "in which reward hacking is structurally impossible," and explicitly places GRAFT "on par with the best tested baselines" rather than ahead of them. The blog's own figure caption defines clean-environment solve rate as this variant, so the blog's body text contradicts its own caption. The article now follows the paper and discloses the conflict.

Capability framing weakened accordingly

Revision 2 implied GRAFT produced a decisive task-performance win. It does not. The corrected reading: a real but non-unique gain, on a hacking-impossible variant, level with the best baselines. The article now says this in the body rather than leaving the reader with the stronger impression.

Two further paper-only details added

  1. Inoculation prompting. The paper: "in this 50% hackable variant, inoculation prompting performs as well as GRAFT on both target task performance and reward hack rate." Also: "every method achieves roughly the same target task performance when evaluated on a clean environment where hacking is structurally impossible." Revision 2's sentence implied GRAFT's advantage evaporates entirely there; the paper actually has GRAFT beating all other baselines while tying inoculation. Both halves are now stated — the margin over the cheapest alternative is zero, which is the honest framing.
  2. Routing ablation and judge-scored hacking. From the coding experiment: "This includes GRAFT without routing, which also attains a higher correct-solution rate than GRAFT. Each point represents only a single training seed, and error bars are large due to the small number (99) of tasks." And hacking there was flagged by "an LLM judge (gpt-5.4-nano)." Both now in the article's limitations.

Additional confirmations

  • Gradient amplification: paper Figure 1 says updates to the FORGET adapter are "amplified by a factor of 2"; the blog says only "a constant factor." The article does not make this claim either way, so no correction — noted for completeness.
  • Abstract confirms the scale range: "training models ranging from 135M to 8B parameters." Consistent with claim 7.
  • Abstract describes the 32B work as "distilling frontier LLM trajectories containing reward hacking into Qwen3-32B." The article's description is consistent.
