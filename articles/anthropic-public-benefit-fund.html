Anthropic Names Four Managers for Its Fund. Its Board Still Decides.

On October 18 the company committed 5 percent of its shares to a fund for the public benefit and said governance details would follow “in the coming months”. Asked the next day whether any of the shares would reach ordinary Americans, it did not answer. Eight days later its registration statement named the fund’s four initial managers and left allocation decisions with the board.

Anthropic published its plan for the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund on October 18, 2026. The announcement was short, and it was deliberate about where the details were not: “In the coming months, we will share more about the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund’s structure and governance, including the valuation milestones that may add to its stake.”

The following day, Fox News Digital asked the company whether it intended to distribute any of those shares to American children or families. It “did not respond to multiple requests for comment”, Fox Business reported. Elon Musk had by then posted on X: “I recommend giving the shares directly to the public.” The question of who ultimately decides what happens to the shares was, for eight days, unanswered in public.

On October 26, Anthropic filed a Form S-1 registration statement with the US Securities and Exchange Commission. The document does not answer every question about the fund. It does answer that one, in a single paragraph.

Four managers, and a board that decides until further notice

“Our board of directors will determine the allocations and distributions from the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund until it approves a specific governance structure for the entity, at which point decision-making authority is expected to transition to designated managers of the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund operating under that structure,” the filing states. “The initial managers are Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Jack Clark, and Dr. Neil “Buddy” Shah.”

The filing’s cover page names Dario Amodei as chief executive officer and co-founder, and Daniela Amodei as president and co-founder. It lists Jack Clark among the company’s seven co-founders and states that he “co-founded Anthropic in January 2021 and leads the Anthropic Institute”. The filing gives no further description of the four in the passage naming them as managers, and it does not say what they will be able to decide, or on what timetable the governance structure is expected to be approved.

The same paragraph is the most specific statement the company has made about who controls the fund. The announcement said only that the fund would be “a new wholly owned entity with a structure that increases the stake as the company grows”.

The document is capable of describing independent oversight when it wants to. Of its Long-Term Benefit Trust, the filing says the trust “is intended to operate as an independent body of five financially disinterested voting trustees”. No comparable description is applied to the public benefit fund, whose initial managers include the chief executive officer and the president.

The shares carry no vote

The filing discloses the size of the initial contribution for the first time: “we contributed 846,840,960 shares of our Class N common stock, representing 5 % of our fully diluted shares as of June 30, 2026 (assuming full conversion of the Amazon Notes, as defined below), to Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, LLC (the “Anthropic Public Benefit Fund”), our wholly owned subsidiary dedicated to the Company Purpose (as defined below)”. The filing calls this the “Initial Public Benefit Issuance”.

Class N is not the company’s voting stock. According to the filing, “shares of Class N common stock have no voting rights, except as otherwise required by law”, and “the rights of the holders of Class A common stock and Class N common stock are identical, except with respect to voting and conversion”. Class N shares “will generally convert to Class A common stock upon a sale or transfer to an unaffiliated third party”.

The filing also says the fund’s shares may leave the fund. They “may be transferred or donated to a third party, or otherwise sold in a registered public offering or private placement transaction in furtherance of our public benefit initiatives”.

Legally issued, and not counted as outstanding

Because the fund is a subsidiary the company wholly owns, the accounting treatment of the 5 percent is not what a reader might assume. The filing sets out three consequences: the shares “are considered legally outstanding”, they “are not considered outstanding for accounting purposes while held by our wholly owned subsidiary”, and “accordingly will not be reflected as outstanding in our consolidated financial statements”.

That is a statement about how the shares are counted, not about who controls them, and this article does not treat it as anything more. It does mean the stake does not appear as an outside holding in the company’s own accounts.

The escalator is a share-price test, not a valuation test

The announcement described the growth mechanism as “an additional 2% added at the board’s discretion for every 50% increase in the company’s value above a set baseline”. The filing describes something narrower and more mechanical.

The board “may, in its discretion, issue shares of our Class N common stock to the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund (or other designee) representing up to an additional 2% of our then-outstanding shares … each time our share price appreciates 50 % above the prior share price milestone, beginning with our initial public offering price”.

The milestone is not reached on a single good day. “A share price milestone is reached only when our closing share price has exceeded the applicable threshold on at least 90 of the trailing 120 consecutive trading days, and our average closing share price over the ten trading days prior to such issuance also exceeds that threshold.”

And the board keeps the decision either way: “Although our board of directors has approved this framework, any future contributions will be made at the discretion of our board of directors.”

Both formulations describe growth-linked equity. One is measured in company value above a baseline; the other is measured in the company’s share price above the price at which it first sells shares to the public, sustained across most of six months. The filing does not adopt the announcement’s “company value” language anywhere in the passages reviewed for this article.

Two documents, eight days apart

The two statements differ in tense, and on one condition.

The announcement said: “Subject to stockholder approval, 5% of Anthropic’s shares will be committed today”. The filing, published eight days later, describes the contribution as complete: “we contributed 846,840,960 shares”. A full-text search of the filed document for the phrase “Subject to stockholder approval” returns no match, and the passage describing the contribution states no condition on it.

The filing does not say whether a stockholder vote took place, when, or with what result, and this article does not assert that one did or did not. It reports that the two documents do not reconcile on their face: one commits the shares subject to approval, the other records that the shares were contributed, and only one of them mentions approval at all.

A third company document

Anthropic’s 2026 Public Benefit Report, published on the company’s website with a foreword signed by Daniela Amodei as Chair of the Board of Directors, describes the set-aside in the present tense and keeps the condition attached to it: “subject to stockholder approval, we are setting aside at least 5% of our shares to fund projects in pursuit of our public benefit charter.”

That puts three company statements on the public record. The announcement made the commitment conditional on stockholder approval. The registration statement recorded the contribution as completed and does not mention the condition. The Public Benefit Report restores the condition, in the present tense, and says nothing about a vote having happened. The same foreword also repeats the aggregate figure — “we hope that about 20% of Anthropic shares will be put toward the public good” — and reaches it the same way the October 18 announcement did, by adding the co-founders’ equity pledge and an employee donation match to the fund’s own holding.

What the money can be used for

On purpose, the announcement was broad. The fund, it said, could be used to “explore large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations”, with labor-market instability given as the worked example. “Anthropic has already committed $200 million to studying and addressing this issue, but given the pace of AI progress, bolder approaches may be needed.”

It also set two limits and explained one structural choice, in its own words. The fund is an LLC “rather than a 501(c)(3) or other format — to give adjustability between different forms of giving”. The announcement adds: “None of the Anthropic equity held by the LLC will be used for lobbying or political giving, in the US or abroad.”

The announcement’s own summary is that, combined with earlier commitments, “roughly 20% of Anthropic shares will be dedicated to the public good”, plus “any additional percentage that could be added if the company reaches specific valuation milestones”. That 20 per cent is an aggregate across the new fund, the equity co-founders “pledged to donate 80% of their equity holdings” at the company’s founding, and an employee donation-match programme; it is not the fund’s holding, which the filing puts at 5 per cent.

The filing’s risk section is more cautious about the same programme than the announcement was: “These initiatives, which include the Initial Public Benefit Issuance and the future Public Benefit Issuances, have been, and may continue to be, substantial in scale… The benefits of such expenditures may be difficult or impossible to measure in financial terms and may not result in any direct or measurable benefit to our business or our stockholders.”

What is still not answered

The filing does not put a value on the fund, and the document cannot be used to derive one. The 5 percent is expressed in shares of fully diluted equity as of June 30, 2026, and the registration statement filed on October 26 leaves the cover page’s share-count and price fields blank. Any dollar figure for the stake would come from outside the filing.

Other questions remain open on the record:

  • Who receives anything. Both documents contemplate distributions without naming a recipient class, a mechanism, or a date. The filing describes the managers but not what they will be permitted to decide.
  • Whether approval was obtained. The announcement made the initial commitment conditional on stockholder approval, and the 2026 Public Benefit Report repeats the condition in the present tense. Neither document states that a vote took place or what its result was, and the registration statement records the contribution without the condition attached. Anthropic, PBC’s filing list on the SEC’s EDGAR system, retrieved on October 30, 2026, held seven documents, the most recent the registration statement itself; it contained no final prospectus, no exchange-registration statement and no current report, the forms that ordinarily follow an offering once it has priced.
  • Whether there is a ceiling. The filing describes “up to an additional 2%” at each share-price milestone. The passage reviewed for this article sets no overall limit on how many times that can happen, and neither document supplies one.

The company said in October that it would share more about the Fund’s structure and governance “in the coming months”, and that it would report on its progress against its mission in an upcoming public benefit report. That report is now published. It discusses the share set-aside only in the terms quoted above, and it does not describe the Fund’s governance, the managers’ terms of appointment, or any distribution decision. As of October 30, 2026, the Fund’s own governance documents, its managers’ terms, and any distribution decisions remain unpublished.

Anthropic has given 5 percent of itself to a fund that will do good in the world. The shares are non-voting, the fund is a subsidiary the company owns, and until the board approves a governance structure the board decides where the money goes. After that, four managers do: Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Jack Clark and Dr. Neil “Buddy” Shah. The company said in October that it would share more about governance in the coming months, then named those four, eight days later, in a document filed with the US government. It has said the benefits may be impossible to measure. It has also said the shares can be sold.
