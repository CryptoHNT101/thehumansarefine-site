Misinformation

One pterodactyl video is fake. The other pterodactyl videos were never independently tested.

By The Humans Are Fine newsroom · Published 26 November 2026

Snopes rated a viral clip of a winged shape over Ciudad Juárez as AI-generated. Its finding covers that single upload. The broadcaster who published the earlier clips now tells subscribers their authenticity is confirmed — and no fact-checker whose work this desk could read has tested them.

READ THE EVIDENCE FILE →

The clip is short, dark and shot from a rooftop. A large winged silhouette crosses the sky above a residential street, wings pointed, beak long. It circulated on Instagram with a caption that travelled with it: “Some Mexican citizens claim to have captured a pterodactyl flying through the night sky over Ciudad Juárez, Chihuahua, Mexico.”

It also circulated on Facebook and Threads, and readers wrote to Snopes asking whether it was real. On 17 November 2026 Snopes published a fact check by Laerke Christensen and rated the claim Fake.

The reasoning is specific, and worth stating exactly. At the start of the video, lettering on a building reads as illegible shapes; a second later the same lettering resolves into text spelling “Jalpan de”. Snopes’s words: “Garbled or gibberish text is a telltale sign that someone used AI to create an image or video.” Printed text does not behave that way in front of a camera. Snopes adds that this is “inconsistent with how printed text behaves in authentic videos”.

Then Snopes drew a boundary around its own finding: “Our assessment of the video above as an AI-generated fake is specific to that individual clip.” Everything that follows depends on that sentence.

What actually spread

There is not one video in circulation. There are several distinct pieces of purported evidence, published by different people on different dates, and they do not stand or fall together. No uploader of any of them has disclosed using AI, and none of the channel pages read for this piece describes its footage as synthetic. This list is what the desk could trace; it makes no claim to be every clip online.

The viral Instagram clip. Origin unknown. Snopes reports that it found versions claiming the clip was recorded on two different dates: “We found versions of the clip claiming it was recorded on both Nov. 14 and 15”. This is the only clip Snopes rated, and the only one for which a production method has been established. Fake — AI-generated or AI-edited.

The Rojas security-camera clip. Published by Maussan Televisión on 5 November 2026 — Snopes names it as its 5 November video in one place and dates the same upload 6 November in another, and this desk could not settle it — in an episode presenting security-camera footage said to have been supplied by a witness named Miguel Rojas and recorded in the San Antonio neighbourhood of Ciudad Juárez. The channel attributes a wingspan estimate of approximately nine metres to a researcher, Marcos Carrera, and a morphological comparison against frigatebirds and pelicans to a biologist, Ricardo Rangel. Neither researcher has been contacted for this piece. Not assessed by Snopes. Production method unresolved.

The Arteaga night-flight clip. Published on 9 November 2026 — though Snopes's citation list dates the same upload 10 November, and this desk could not settle it — and described by the channel as its third piece of evidence. The episode is headlined as the best image ever captured of the Mothman, and it presents footage said to have been recorded by a boy, Fernando Arteaga, in the Felipe Ángeles neighbourhood. Snopes reports the boy told the channel’s researcher that the creature tried to attack a dog before releasing it — and immediately qualifies that: “This reported encounter was not part of the video Maussan shared.” The dog attack is testimony, not footage. Not assessed by Snopes. Production method unresolved.

The trail-camera footage and the still photograph. The same episodes carry camera-trap footage said to show a fleeing animal, and a still image said to show a winged figure with glowing eyes. A different animal, and a still rather than a recording; neither should be counted as a pterosaur sighting. Not assessed. Production method unresolved.

What the city is reported to have said

Snopes reports that Sergio Rodríguez, head of Civil Protection in Ciudad Juárez, told local media the videos were “solely for digital entertainment and lack any real basis”, and that if the creature were real there would be an investigation by Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History — which, he said, there was not.

That quotation has not been verified, by Snopes or by us. Snopes states that it contacted Rodríguez to check the quote and contacted the institute for its assessment, and was awaiting replies at publication. This desk could not retrieve the local reports behind it. It is reported speech about reported speech, and the piece says so rather than presenting it as the city’s settled position.

Why appearance is not evidence

Whether the silhouette looks like a pterosaur, a heron, or a pterosaur badly drawn by a model settles nothing. A shape in a low-resolution night video carries no information about the process that produced it. Nor does an AI-detector score, which is a model’s confidence about a file rather than an observation of how it was made — and no detector score appears in anything examined for this piece.

What Snopes used was a physical inconsistency, printed text mutating on a wall, characteristic of generative video. That is evidence. Neither the look of the thing nor a percentage from a classifier is.

The claim did not stop

On 23 November 2026, six days after the fact check, the channel published an episode asserting that the authenticity of the video recorded by a child had been confirmed. The episode names the boy as Isaac Fernando Arteaga, aged 10, and says the programme reviews the technical verification of the original file’s metadata, a forensic acoustic analysis, and a second security-camera recording.

The chapter list shows those segments exist. The published description contains no method, no named analyst for the metadata work, and no report. Whether that verification means anything cannot be judged from outside the programme. The authenticity of any Maussan clip remains unestablished.

A count worth being precise about: Snopes records that the journalist “shared three clips” from 5 November onward. This desk’s own catalogue runs to five items — counting the trail-camera footage carried inside those episodes — but they are not five of the same kind. Three are clips distributed by that channel, one is the separate viral clip Snopes rated, and one is a still photograph. The comparison is between things that look alike and are not.

The biology is not in dispute. The Natural History Museum notes that “Pterosaurs were around until the end of the Cretaceous Period”, around 66 million years ago, and that they were not dinosaurs at all. The broadcaster’s own material calls its subject a Pteranodon rather than a pterodactyl, a distinction the museum's own page makes in passing.

What remains unresolved

  • Who made the viral clip, and when. Snopes found no source and two competing recording dates. Nothing has changed that.
  • How the Rojas, Arteaga and trail-camera material was produced. No one whose work this desk could read has assessed it — not Snopes, not any authority we could find. Unresolved is the accurate word, not also fake.
  • Whether the pieces are independent. Several are distributed by the same channel that is making the authenticity claim. Whether the witnesses found the channel or the channel found the witnesses is not established.
  • What changed between uploads. Cropping, re-captioning, language, overlays and speed were not compared: no clip file was obtained, and the only frames seen were the two stills embedded in the fact check.
  • The provenance of the original Arteaga file. Metadata verification is asserted, not published.
  • What the municipal government actually said, and whether the institute examined anything. Unverified through two removes.
  • Whether the nine-metre wingspan estimate survives scrutiny. It is attributed to a researcher appearing on the programme that broadcast the footage; no independent measurement exists in anything retrieved.

A note on sourcing

This account rests substantially on one fact check, Snopes, plus the broadcaster’s own published video pages, read directly. The single independent institutional voice in it, the Natural History Museum, speaks only to extinction. Where that is thin, the piece says so rather than filling the gap.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom’s own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

The interesting thing here is not that a pterodactyl video is fake. It is that only one of them was ever tested, and the person publishing the others has now told a large audience that the testing came back clean.

That is how a correction gets laundered. A fact-checker draws a careful boundary around a single upload, a broadcaster steps over the boundary, and the audience hears that the whole genre has been vindicated. Nobody lied about the test. The test simply was not the test anyone now thinks it was.

Watch the shapes, not the silhouettes. A fake video and an unchecked video are two different things, and only one of them has been looked at.
