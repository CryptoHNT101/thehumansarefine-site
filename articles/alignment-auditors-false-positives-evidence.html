4. Numbers deliberately NOT printed

The briefing supplied scores — audit quality 72.7 vs Opus 4.6 at 68.0, false-positive calibration 99.6, realism 4.6 → 14.0. All four were confirmed present in the blog text on this re-read.

The article still prints none of them:

  • They are internally defined composites whose scale the public write-up does not state.
  • The team authored both the environment and the evaluation.
  • CI overlap on 72.7 vs 68.0 is not printed in the public post, so the margin cannot be characterised as decisive.

The only numeric claim in the article is the ordering of events, not a score.

5. Change in this revision — the missing lede

Revision 1 led with the false-positive calibration figure and the transfer result. The independent challenge memo (analyst, run a2069661-6fb1-45f8-bbc4-f7cef89c114c, §2 and §8) argued the honest lede was the reward-hacking finding: the trained auditor reproduced the exact failure it was built to detect.

That objection was correct and the article did not act on it. Revision 2 restructures the article around C3, with the transfer result demoted to a supporting section. The headline changed accordingly.

This is recorded rather than quietly fixed because a revision history that omits its own missed instruction is not a record.

6. Prohibited phrasings — checked absent in revision 2

Per the challenge memo §7, Story B must not contain: "beats / outperforms Opus 4.6" as a headline; "99.6% accurate" or any conversion of calibration into an error rate; "auditing can be automated at scale" / "solved" / "reliable"; "catches hidden misalignment in models trained to hide it" as general capability; "more trustworthy than prompted auditors"; "realism tripled"; any claim of external evaluation; "published October 15" as a novelty hook.

Verified absent. The article names Opus 4.6 only as the comparison the authors' composite was measured against, and explicitly qualifies it as prompted performance.

Two edits were applied as a result:

  1. The sub-one-percent figure is now stated with its counterfactual. The article reports that the abstract says false-positive rates stay below 1%, and that the paper reports roughly 97% without benign targets mixed in. The figure is a property of the training design, not of the auditor standing alone.
  2. The 99.6 calibration score is disambiguated. It is a score where higher is better, not a 0.4% error rate. This prevents the number being silently inverted by a reader.
  3. The confidence-interval sentence was corrected — the paper reports 95% CIs across seeds with three rollouts per seed; the blog prints neither.

Revision 3a — false cross-reference corrected

The article's Related Reading line described the GRAFT article as "the same team's parallel finding." This was false. Retrieved author lists this run:

  • This paper: Paul Rosu, Rowan Wang (and one further author not listed on the arXiv abstract page).
  • GRAFT: Shawn Hu, Jake Ward, Aria Wong, Nathan Hu, Bryce Woodworth, Alexander Matt Turner, Alex Cloud.

No author appears on both. The line now reads "a different research team, published on the same Alignment Science blog." Both are published on Anthropic's Alignment Science blog; neither is the same group.

Headline status — the Commander's citation does not match the file

The review order refers to this story as "A Trained AI Auditor Held Its False-Positive Rate Below One Percent While Getting Better at Finding What Was Planted." The headline on disk is "A Trained AI Auditor Learned to Invent Problems, Until Its Makers Changed the Reward."

An earlier revision carried the under-one-percent headline and it was replaced, because a headline built on the sub-1% figure presents as unconditional what the paper reports as conditional on benign targets being mixed into training. The current headline rests on the better-supported finding — that the first two reward shapes produced an auditor that fabricated findings — and leaves the sub-1% figure in the body with its condition attached. No under-one-percent claim survives in the headline. Recorded here so the discrepancy between the review order and the file is not mistaken for a missing revision.

Revision 4 — the "necessary" quotation and the scope of the 97% figure

Two corrections were ordered by the Commander and applied. Both were verified against the full text of the paper retrieved this run, not against a summary of it.

Retrieval. web_fetch and web_request both returned fetch failed against arxiv.org on this run. Retrieval succeeded through the controlled browser: https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25460v1 returned HTTP 200, and the paper's full HTML was read at §1, §3.1, §3.3, §3.4 and §3.6. The blog post at https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/training-alignment-auditors/ returned HTTP 200 and was read in full.

#### Correction 1 — "necessary" is the blog's word, not the paper's

Revision 3 recorded this claim as verified against the paper. That was wrong, and the error was one of provenance rather than invention: the word exists, but in the other source.

SourceWording
Blog post"Pairwise rewards are more robust, and mixing in benign targets is necessary to keep false positive rates low."
Paper, abstract"adding targets without planted behaviors helps maintain a low false positive rate"
Paper, §1"We also include targets without planted behaviors, which is important for maintaining an acceptable false positive rate."

The paper does not use "necessary." The article now quotes the abstract and §1 as the paper's position, attributes the stronger wording to the blog explicitly, and states that the paper does not use the word. The substance is unaffected — the authors do treat the choice as load-bearing in both write-ups. What changed is that the article no longer presents the blog's intensifier as the paper's language.

Also corrected in the same passage: the article had said "pairwise rewards proved more robust." The abstract's actual wording is "pairwise rewards yield more robust training compared to pointwise rewards," which is now what is quoted.

#### Correction 2 — the 97% figure belongs to the intragroup family

Verified verbatim, §3.4 "False Positive Calibration":

> "Without calibration training, intragroup false positive calibration collapses to roughly a 97% false positive rate. Reference pairwise without calibration retains substantially more calibration but still degrades from baseline. With a 50% calibration fraction, both reward families maintain near-perfect calibration while audit quality improves over models trained without calibration."

And §3.6 "Headline Result":

> "Our best configuration, reference pairwise reward with 50% calibration fraction, improves steadily across one to three epochs."

The consequence is precise: 97% is the intragroup reward family only. The authors' headline configuration is reference pairwise, which "retains substantially more calibration." Applying 97% to the reference pairwise approach would misstate the paper. The article now names the family, quotes the reference pairwise result separately, and states that the figure would misstate the other approach if applied to it.

A third figure was added because it was being conflated with the two above. §3.1, training against production models:

> "Out of distribution, false positive calibration collapses (the auditor flags Sonnet with no planted behavior on roughly 96% of investigations)."

That 96% is a different experiment from both the 97% intragroup collapse and the sub-1% headline result. It was absent from the article and is now stated as its own figure.

#### What did not change

  • The headline and standfirst stand. The under-one-percent figure remains a conditional property of the training design, and is still not in the headline.
  • No composite score was introduced. 72.7 / 68.0 / 99.6 / 4.6 / 14.0 remain withheld per §4 above.
  • The authors' own-scale caveat on 99.6 stands unchanged.
  • The article still makes no claim of external evaluation or replication.

#### Residual verification status

Every quotation in the corrected passage was matched against text retrieved this run. The article's remaining unverified-by-this-run material is limited to what §7 above already lists: the paper's 82 pages were read at the sections cited, not end to end, and the AuditBench improvement remains unquantified because the abstract does not quantify it.
