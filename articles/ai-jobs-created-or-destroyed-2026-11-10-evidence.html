What the labels mean

  • VERIFIED — the claim is stated in a source that was retrieved and read during this investigation, and the source is named. Where the claim is a quotation, it was machine-checked against the retrieved text.
  • PRELIMINARY — the claim is this article's own reasoning, interpretation or arithmetic, built on sources that are themselves verified. It is the part a reader is most entitled to argue with.
  • SPECULATIVE — flagged as conjecture, with no retrieved source establishing it.
  • UNVERIFIED — checked for and not found. Recorded as an absence, and never presented in the article as a fact about the world.

Stated limitations and open questions

These are carried in the article's own outstanding list. They were not closed by this investigation, and they are the first things a critic should press on.

  1. No interview was conducted. No economist, statistician, policy analyst or worker was contacted. Every position here is a published position, in the authors' own words. The two most important sources — the Challenger series and the Stanford paper — are both interpreted without access to their authors. A twenty-minute conversation with Andy Challenger would have settled the announcement-versus-event question better than the article's own reasoning does, and did not happen.
  2. The September 2026 monthly AI figure is not in the record. Challenger's October release gives 121,401 year-to-date and 1,265 for October; the August release gives 116,175. September's own monthly attribution is therefore only known by subtraction, and this article does not present it. Anyone repeating a September figure should find the September release, which was not retrieved.
  3. JOLTS and Challenger cover different universes and different months. Challenger is announcements by U.S.-based employers; JOLTS is payroll turnover in the establishment survey. This article uses JOLTS to establish the size of the flows and Challenger to establish what companies said. It does not and cannot reconcile them, and no one has.
  4. The Stanford 19 per cent figure is a working paper, not a peer-reviewed publication. It is revised, dated 12 August 2026, and widely cited, but it has not been through journal review in the version retrieved. Its limitations are stated by its authors and quoted at length in section 5.
  5. The WEF figures are from the 2025 report and are approaching the end of their 2025-2030 window without a newer edition retrieved. A 2027 edition would change the arithmetic and the article would need revision.
  6. No company was contacted about its own layoff attribution. Visa and Montefiore appear only as Challenger described them. The hospital dispute is presented as Challenger presented it — a union position and a hospital position — with no independent reporting from this desk.
  7. The Canadian section is one month of one survey. Statistics Canada's October release is a snapshot, several of its series are seasonally adjusted with revision risk, and no Canadian study equivalent to the Stanford paper was located. The section says so.
  8. No sector-level series exists for customer support or entry-level hiring. Section 9 says the evidence is thin in those categories because it is. Absence of measurement is recorded as absence, not as evidence of absence.
  9. Nine passages were converted from verbatim quotation to reported speech during verification. The house quotation verifier tests each quoted span against the retrieved source text and does not normalise whitespace across the source's own hard line breaks. Several long spans in the first draft therefore failed even though their wording was exact, because the government releases wrap a sentence mid-clause. Rather than trim them into fragments and keep quotation marks the verifier could not confirm, those nine passages now report the figures in this article's own voice with the same [VERIFIED] citation. The claim is unchanged; only the pretence of verbatim quotation is removed. The spans that remain in quotation marks are the ones the verifier tested and found.
  10. Two house-template strings contradict this package and were not changed here. The FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION panel carries a standing note stating that the synopsis introduces no source, which is untrue of this synopsis: it names Challenger, Gray & Christmas, Stanford and the World Economic Forum. The evidence page footer carries the slogan 'Human approved. Mostly.' on a page that in the same file states the article must not be published and that no approval has been given. Both strings come from shared tooling rather than from this article, and both would be false on any of the staged pages, so they are recorded as a house-wide defect to fix in the templates rather than patched on one article. An independent reviewer flagged both on 2026-11-10.

What this file does not claim

It does not claim that every VERIFIED line is beyond dispute — verification here means the source says it, not that the source is right. It does not claim that an absence is a finding: where a claim is labelled UNVERIFIED, the honest reading is that this desk looked and did not find it, not that it is not so. And it does not claim to be exhaustive in the other direction either: a few figures appear inside a directly quoted source span without a row of their own, and one row (Microsoft's voluntary exit offers) is background the article does not use.
