Instagram Will Now Guess How Old You Are. It Can Overrule the Birthday You Typed.

By BARBARA WALTERS, accountability desk · AI-drafted · desk assignment, not human authorship

READ THE EVIDENCE FILE →

Meta announced on 9 November that it is expanding its AI age-estimation technology globally on Instagram, to place what it describes as "millions more teens" into the stricter account settings the company calls Teen Accounts. TechCrunch reported the expansion, including the Teen Account defaults and the August settlements. The description of the technique, including the "height or bone structure" wording, circulated in early November coverage and was later withdrawn - the withdrawal note is quoted below. Meta had published that language itself in May 2026.

The mechanism is the part worth understanding, because it inverts how age has worked online. Until now, the birthday you typed in was the default signal. Meta's 5 May post describes one escalation that already exists: when it suspects someone is misrepresenting their age - for example by changing a birthday from under 18 to over 18 - it requires them to verify with an ID or Yoti's facial age estimation tools before the change completes. The system described here does not wait to be told.

"We use AI technology to find people we suspect to be teens, even if they use an adult birthday, and place them in Teen Account protections."

The company's announcement sets out the intent plainly:

"Based on that success, today we're expanding this technology globally."

What the system looks at

Earlier coverage described the system as analysing an entire profile for contextual clues - mentions of school grades, birthday celebrations - and as reading visual cues from photos and videos, including what those reports called bone-structure analysis. Two separate retractions are at work here and they should not be collapsed into one. Engadget's 9 November note withdrew both elements as descriptions of how Meta estimates a teenager's age - the bone-structure analysis and the mentions of school grades or birthday celebrations - recording Meta's clarification that those measures are used to detect and remove people aged under 13. Separately, Meta's own live 5 May post still carries the profile-contextual-clue language, and no longer contains the height or bone-structure sentence, and the 31 July correction on that page concerned biometric data and facial recognition. Engadget's update note of 9 November states: "A previous version of this story indicated that Meta is using bone structure analysis of photos, as well as mentions of things like school grades or birthday celebrations to estimate a person's age. The company clarified to Engadget that these measures are used to detect and remove people aged under 13 from its platforms."

That detail is Meta's own, and how it changed is more revealing than the November coverage suggested. Meta published it on 5 May 2026, in the post announcing AI visual analysis. The sentence is no longer in the live post, but the archived version of that post, captured on 3 June 2026, reads:

"We want to be clear: this is not facial recognition. Our AI looks at general themes and visual cues, for example height or bone structure, to estimate someone's general age; it does not identify the specific person in the image."

No document reviewed for this article states when or why it was removed. Separately, on 31 July 2026 the post was updated with a correction stating that the AI "does not use biometric data to identify whether a person is underage", that it "does not create facial fingerprints or identify who is in a photo", and that it is "trained to recognize general visual patterns that suggest someone who is underage is in the photo." The wording survives in the archived version, and in early November coverage published before it was withdrawn.

The detail is not new: Meta described the signal in its May 2026 post, the live post no longer carries that description, and the November coverage carried the earlier detail forward. This publication found no document stating when, or on what grounds, that description was removed, and the 31 July correction does not mention it. Meta has since narrowed it further on the record: asked about the bone-structure and profile-clue measures on 9 November, the company told Engadget that they are used to detect and remove people under 13, and did not say they play no part in the 13-17 estimate. Nothing in the November announcement repeats the visual-analysis detail, and Meta has not said whether any visual signal contributes to the 13-17 estimates used in the global expansion. The live page now says that the AI looks at "visual cues in a photo or video" and does not identify who is in it - a narrower claim than the one it replaced. The correction is stated in terms of biometric data and facial recognition.

Neither document reviewed for this article says which model performs this analysis, what its error rates are, or what a user wrongly placed in a teen account can do to correct that placement. Its stated basis for confidence is directional rather than numerical:

"Since first introducing this technology, we've been placing more teens in age-appropriate protections and are encouraged by the results."

"Encouraged by the results" is not a false-positive rate. Neither the announcement nor the blog post reviewed for this article reports one — no accuracy figure, no share of adults wrongly placed into a teen experience, and no count of appeals. That absence is the most significant thing in the disclosure.

What happens when the system guesses "teen"

The consequences are not cosmetic. Teen Accounts, as described by TechCrunch, apply a stricter set of defaults: direct messages only from people the account follows or is already connected to, harmful comments hidden, and the account set to private by default.

For an actual teenager, that is the intended protection. For a 25-year-old who is miscategorised — and nothing in the documents reviewed for this article indicates how often that happens — it means a stranger can no longer message them, their account defaults to private, and comments begin disappearing. The user may not be told why, and may have no obvious route to correct it.

That is the trade the feature makes, and the documents reviewed for this article do not publish the data required to evaluate it.

Why Meta is doing this now

The announcement presents the expansion as building on commitments made under a legal agreement rather than as a purely product decision.

According to TechCrunch, Meta reached settlements in August with nearly every US state and territory, worth up to $18 billion, resolving lawsuits alleging its platforms harm teens, and agreed as part of them to improve how it identifies teens and children under 13. Meta's own announcement confirms an agreement with "52 bipartisan attorneys general" across states, territories and the District of Columbia, but gives no date and no sum. This publication has not reviewed a settlement document.

The $18 billion figure is therefore unverified rather than disputed: it is reported by TechCrunch, and in the same terms by Engadget, but no settlement document has been reviewed, and Meta's own announcement gives no total. It is presented as approximate and attributed for that reason alone.

The company's blog post links the two directly:

"That's why as part of our agreement with 52 bipartisan attorneys general across US states, territories, and the District of Columbia, we're investing in even stronger technology to proactively catch accounts that may belong to children under 13."

Read the sequence with that in mind. A regulator required better age identification; a company built an AI system to provide it; and that system now applies globally, including in markets where this publication found no comparable agreement. This publication found no indication in the documents reviewed that any regulator outside the United States required this expansion. Meta presents the worldwide rollout as flowing from its US agreement, and does not say whether rules in other markets also require it — and in other markets where governments are moving to require age assurance for minors, the question is not academic.

The same blog post states the general commitment:

"We'll continue to do our part to proactively ensure teens are in age-appropriate experiences on our apps."

Where else this is going

The expansion is staged. Meta says it is continuing to roll the technology out on Facebook, and that "later this year, we'll begin launching similar technology globally on Threads."

It is also adding a second, entirely different signal in one market. In Australia, Meta says it has begun using Apple's age-range signal "as another method to help understand age" - a device-level declaration passed from the app store to the developer, rather than an inference from behaviour or appearance. Meta is explicit about the limits, and about its own motive: "We're voluntarily using this signal, but as long as collecting it and sharing it remains optional for app stores, and using it remains optional for developers, teens will receive inconsistent protections across the many apps they use." That is the company arguing a useful input is not a sufficient one, and calling for legislation to make it universal. It is a different claim from whether one signal is more accurate than another, which the announcement does not address.

Alongside the rollout, Meta says parents in the United States will begin seeing an in-feed video on Instagram this month explaining its approach, linking to an article by Larry Magid, president and chief executive of the internet-safety organisation ConnectSafely.

Why this matters to a reader who is not a parent

Two things, and neither is about teenagers.

The first is that age verification on the internet is being rebuilt around inference rather than declaration. The birthday field was always fiction, and everyone knew it. What replaces it is a system that estimates your age from signals Meta does not describe — and, for catching under-13s, from what Meta says its AI can see in a photo. That estimate now determines what your account can do. The same technique, once built, is available for any other classification a platform or a government might want to make.

The second is that the accuracy question is being answered without evidence. Meta says the results encouraged it. It does not say in either document what they were. If the system is highly accurate, publishing the error rate would support the rollout. If it is not, the people affected — adults locked into teenager settings — are bearing a cost the company has not quantified in public.

What the announcement does not say

No accuracy or false-positive figures. No description of how a user learns that they have been estimated rather than believed, or how they appeal. No statement of how the 13-17 estimate is produced, or whether any visual signal feeds it in any market. No retention detail for the visual analysis, beyond the company's general privacy terms. No independent audit of the system's performance. And no indication that the global expansion was required by any regulator outside the United States.

The fair reading

Catching adults who claim to be 14, and catching 13-year-olds who claim to be 25, is a real problem, and one that has survived more than a decade of work on it — the timescale Meta itself gives, dating its work on teen protections to 'more than a decade' — with the harms of the gap falling on children. Meta is now spending real engineering on it, under legal pressure, and the direction of the change is toward protecting the younger user.

It is also true that the method has been expanded worldwide with no published error rate, on signals users do not know are being read, with consequences that fall hardest on whoever the model gets wrong — and that the company's evidence for its own accuracy is a sentence about being encouraged.

Both of those are true at once. That is the honest state of the thing.

Instagram is now going to guess how old you are.

Not ask. Guess. You can type any birthday you like, and the computer will look at whatever it looks at, and decide for itself whether you are really a teenager. For a grown-up who gets guessed wrong, it is just confusing. And here is the fun part: Instagram has not said how often it guesses wrong. It says it is "encouraged by the results", which is what you say when you do not want to show the numbers.
