Outlook and Copilot Went Down Together. Microsoft Has Not Said AI Was the Cause

An Exchange Online outage took Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and Microsoft 365 Copilot offline for hours on Thursday. Microsoft's own incident records describe overloaded servers, a process that handles secure connections becoming overwhelmed and a network configuration change the company has begun reverting, and name no AI workload as the cause.

A widespread Microsoft 365 outage left users unable to reach their mailboxes for hours on Thursday, and the company's own status updates place the failure inside its server fleet rather than in anything those servers were being asked to do with artificial intelligence.

Exchange Online began degrading at 16:17 UTC (11:17 a.m. Eastern), according to Microsoft's incident record EX1550543. The broader Microsoft 365 incident, MO1550821, opened at 18:26 UTC (1:26 p.m. Eastern) and covers the knock-on impact across other services. Neither incident had ended at the time of writing, and both remained classified as service degradations rather than resolved.

What Microsoft says failed

The Exchange Online record is titled, in Microsoft's words, “Users may be unable to connect to their mailboxes in Exchange Online through multiple connection methods”. The potentially impacted routes it lists are Outlook on the web, the Messaging API known as MAPI, Exchange ActiveSync and the REST API.

By 22:00 UTC Microsoft had identified a mechanism. Its update states that “a core process on the affected servers is becoming overwhelmed, which causes the servers to stop responding and contributes to the capacity loss”. Earlier the same afternoon the wider Microsoft 365 record, MO1550821, had described the visible symptom as “elevated load across multiple regions, which is likely contributing to the connectivity errors”, and the Exchange Online record had already said the load was “contributing to the connectivity errors users are receiving”. Both are earlier statements, and both are hedged about mechanism.

Those two statements describe a cascade rather than a single fault. Servers stopped responding, capacity fell, and the servers still working absorbed the traffic. Microsoft's own update states that “the elevated load isn't driven by an overall increase in traffic, but by a large number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable”, while an earlier update said it was “reviewing an unexpected increase in a specific type of service traffic” to establish whether one kind of request was contributing. SecurityWeek's reporting, which attributes the no-increase finding to Microsoft, is consistent with the record.

At 01:06 UTC on 13 November Microsoft narrowed the mechanism further, and this is the most specific cause-adjacent statement it has released. Its update states that “a process on the affected servers responsible for establishing secure connections becomes overwhelmed, causing the servers to stop processing requests”. The same update says the company has “begun reverting a recent network configuration change that correlates with the start of impact”. It is monitoring to determine whether the revert contributes to recovery.

Two things should be kept apart in that sentence. A correlation with the start of impact is a timing relationship, which is a reason to revert a change and watch what happens. It is not a declaration that the change caused the outage, and Microsoft has not made one. Earlier, at 19:57 UTC, the Exchange Online record said: “we haven't identified any recent changes that correlate with the start of impact”. Both statements are accurate as of their own timestamps, and the difference between them is an investigation moving, not a record contradicting itself.

The recovery path ran into the same problem repeatedly. At 20:38 UTC Microsoft reported that “many of the affected servers have become unresponsive, which is preventing us from confirming whether the change is effective”. It validated an alternative health check method, and said it was preparing an automated process to detect and restart unresponsive servers. It also said it was rebuilding a subset of affected servers using “a previous software configuration” and would widen that approach if it worked.

Why Copilot went down with the email

The service Microsoft lists as Microsoft Copilot 365 appears in the affected list for MO1550821, alongside Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint Online, Defender XDR, Planner, To Do, Intune, Defender for Office 365, several Purview services, Power BI, the Microsoft 365 Apps Admin Center, Universal Print, Windows 365, and the services added in the 00:00 and 00:45 UTC revisions: the Microsoft 365 admin center, Microsoft 365 apps, Microsoft 365 Education, Microsoft Graph and Microsoft Fabric.

Microsoft also sets out what Copilot's failure looked like for a user, in the Copilot line of that same list. The advisory states: “Users may be unable to load Copilot Chat or receive responses in the web and desktop apps, and Copilot experiences may be unable to access work data such as email, calendar, and files.” That is a description of what a user lost, not of what broke.

The advisory explains the relationship directly. Microsoft writes that “Features of the Microsoft 365 services below that interact with Exchange Online and email services are impacted. Elements of those services that do not depend on interaction with Exchange Online remain functional.”

That is a dependency statement, not a causal one. Copilot was listed because it reads mail and documents that live in the systems that were failing. Some Google Workspace users also reported trouble reaching their Google-hosted mail through Outlook desktop and mobile clients, per SecurityWeek, which points the same way: the client and the assistant are only as available as the mail system underneath them.

Did AI cause it?

Not on the evidence Microsoft has published. Both incident records carry a Root Cause field reading N/A at their last update, which means the company has not yet published a root cause at all. No AI workload, model, training run, inference cluster or AI agent is named anywhere in the advisories. Microsoft's updates describe four threads: an overwhelmed core process, a more specific process on the affected servers responsible for establishing secure connections becoming overwhelmed, a recent network configuration change being reverted because it correlates with the start of impact, and an unexpected increase in one type of service traffic. None of the four is an AI workload, and the correlation is the strongest link Microsoft has published to anything external.

The word automation does appear, but not as a culprit. The suspect component is the one that processes health checks, an ordinary piece of infrastructure monitoring, and Microsoft's response was to add more automation, not less: an automated process to continually detect and restart unresponsive servers. Nothing in the record describes a model making a decision. Automated server monitoring and artificial intelligence are different things, and the advisories describe only the former.

The honest answer is narrower than a clean no. There is no disclosed evidence that AI infrastructure or AI-assisted operations caused this outage, and the mechanism Microsoft describes is conventional server capacity loss. But because no root cause has been published, an AI role is not formally excluded either. It is simply unevidenced.

One further question is open and should not be overstated. Microsoft released its November Patch Tuesday updates two days before the outage, addressing 1,094 vulnerabilities, including an Outlook spoofing flaw, CVE-2026-124194, that Microsoft says has been exploited. SecurityWeek reports that Microsoft's service advisories have not linked that vulnerability to the Exchange Online disruption. Two events close in time are not thereby connected.

What is not known, and what to watch

Microsoft has not explained why the secure-connection process became overwhelmed, why the health checks failed, why processor usage rose on the affected servers, or what the network configuration change it is reverting actually changed. On malicious activity Microsoft has now said something explicit. Its 03:34 UTC update on 13 November states: “for now we can confirm that there is no indication this is the result of malicious activity”. That is the company speaking for itself, and it is a firmer statement than SecurityWeek's reporting had been able to make earlier. It answers the security question without answering the causation question: the same update still publishes no root cause and names no AI workload, and saying there is no indication of malicious activity is not the same as saying what did happen.

This article covers Exchange Online, the business mail service, as described in Microsoft's own incident records. It does not establish that the consumer services Outlook.com or Hotmail were affected, and the incident text does not claim they were.

At the last updates read for this article, 03:34:14 UTC for EX1550543 and 03:37:38 UTC for MO1550821, Microsoft promised further updates at 04:30 UTC, and a post-incident report is the document that would settle causation. Until it appears, the defensible reading is that this was a cloud infrastructure failure that also disrupted AI products, not an AI failure that disrupted a cloud.

That distinction matters commercially. Assistants are sold as an overlay on mail, documents and chat. When the layer beneath them fails, the assistant fails with it, and every vendor shipping an assistant on someone else's storage inherits that exposure.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom's own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

On Thursday afternoon a lot of people tried to open their email and could not. Outlook said no. Teams got wobbly. Copilot, the friendly AI helper that lives inside Microsoft's apps, stopped helping too.

Here is the useful part: Copilot did not break. Copilot needs your email and your files to do its job, and the place where the email lives was broken. So the helper went down with the mail, like a lamp going out because somebody switched off the power strip.

Microsoft says some of its computers got too busy, stopped answering, and left the rest of the computers doing too much work. It has not said a robot did that. It has not said what did it yet.

So did AI break Microsoft? Nobody has shown that, and the company's own notes point at ordinary server trouble. The humans are still checking. The Humans Are Fine.
