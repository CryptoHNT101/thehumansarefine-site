UK Lab Restores Filtered Protein Model Capability for Under $80

The UK AI Security Institute reports that removing viral sequences from protein language model training data suppressed the targeted ability without significantly damaging neighbouring ones — and that the withheld ability could then be restored for under $80 of compute. The authors say that is not enough to call filtering a safeguard on its own.

The UK AI Security Institute reports that filtering viral sequences out of the data used to train protein language models suppressed the targeted capability without significantly damaging adjacent ones — and that the withheld capability could then be recovered at relatively low cost.

In a blog post published 26 October 2026, the institute’s Chem-Bio team describes work in which it pretrained ESM-2 protein language models from scratch, with viral sequences removed from the training data. According to the post, the team conducted training at several scales, ranging from 8M to 3B parameters, to test whether the effect of filtering held as models grew.

The result the authors report is selective: the targeted capability was suppressed, while adjacent capabilities were not significantly impacted. That is the outcome a data-filtering safeguard is designed to produce.

The authors do not treat that result as sufficient. In their summary of the findings, they state that filtering “does not constitute a robust safeguard on its own, since the lost capability can be recovered at relatively low cost (under $80 of compute even for our largest model), and the data required to do so is often publicly available and easy to find.”

The $80 figure is attached, in the post’s own wording, to the largest model in the study, which corresponds to the top of the stated 8M to 3B parameter range. The post attributes the low recovery cost to the availability of the necessary data: the material needed to restore the capability is, in the team’s account, often public and easy to locate.

The authors’ conclusion is that filtering remains worth doing but cannot stand alone. They write that “for this to be an effective safeguard, it needs to come alongside other interventions, such as data access controls.” The framing places filtering as one component of a wider set of controls rather than a barrier that holds by itself.

The post also situates the work within the current generation of safeguards. The authors describe stringent filtering of pre-training data as “one of the most promising” of the safeguards developed to date — a characterisation that sits alongside their own finding that it is not durable by itself.

For policy, the practical implication the authors draw concerns where to place weight. If a capability removed at training time can be restored with a small amount of compute against public data, then the durability of the safeguard depends on factors outside the training run — specifically, on who can obtain the data needed for recovery. That is the gap the authors point to when they call for data access controls alongside filtering.

The study’s scope is protein language models, not frontier general-purpose language models. The post describes the work as pretraining ESM-2 models and reporting results across scales from 8M to 3B parameters; it does not claim the same recovery economics apply to other model classes.

The recovery method described is fine-tuning on publicly available data. The authors do not present recovery as requiring new capability development, but as restoration of something the filtering had removed, using data that is often publicly accessible.

The blog post announces an accompanying paper. That paper was not independently read in the course of preparing this report; figures and characterisations beyond the quoted passages are taken from the blog’s own summary of the work. The finding rests on a single institutional source, with no independent replication reported.

The institute is the UK AI Security Institute, an official body of the UK government. The post is attributed to its Chem-Bio team.

What the record establishes is narrower than a general claim about AI safety: a government lab filtered viral sequences from protein language model training data, observed selective suppression of the targeted capability, and then restored that capability for under $80 of compute using data it describes as often publicly available. The authors’ own conclusion is that filtering is a partial measure that requires complementary controls.

FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

Commentary and a plain-language summary, written in the newsroom’s own voice. It is not reporting, it introduces no source, and it should not be quoted as a finding.

Good news: we built a filter. Bad news: we also built the receipt.

A UK government lab, the AI Security Institute, trained protein language models from scratch after removing viral sequences from the data. It worked, in the narrow sense that the thing they wanted gone was gone, and the neighbouring abilities were mostly fine. Filtering is, in their own words, one of the most promising safeguards developed to date.

Then they put the missing ability back for under $80 of compute. On their biggest model. Using data that is, per their own summary, often publicly available and easy to find.

So the filter held until somebody with a laptop and a small budget decided it should not. The authors say this plainly: it is not a robust safeguard on its own, and needs to come alongside other interventions, such as data access controls. That is a government research team telling you the lock works and the door is optional.

We are told the machinery of safety is layered, redundant and thoughtfully designed. It is, in the sense that a screen door is a layer. The screen is real. The mosquitoes are also real. And the cost of opening the screen has now been published, with a number attached, in a format anyone can cite.
